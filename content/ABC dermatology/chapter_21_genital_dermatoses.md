* **Identify these lesions as Angiokeratomas, which appear as multiple, dark red to purple papules on the vulva or scrotum.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** These are benign vascular lesions formed by the dilation of superficial capillaries in the dermis combined with epidermal thickening (hyperkeratosis). While they mimic a rash or melanoma due to their dark color, they are non-infectious and usually asymptomatic, though they may bleed if scratched during grooming.

![[page612_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Acute Allergic Contact Dermatitis by the presence of intense, ill-defined erythema (redness) and edema.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** This is a Type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction mediated by T-cells. In the genital area, common culprits include fragrances (in soaps/wipes), preservatives, topical medications (like neomycin or antifungals), or latex; unlike irritant dermatitis, this reaction requires prior sensitization to the allergen.

![[page613_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Lichen Simplex Chronicus by observing lichenification (thickening of the skin) and excoriations (scratch marks).**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** This is not a primary disease but a secondary response to the "itch-scratch cycle." Chronic rubbing causes the skin to defend itself by thickening, which exaggerates normal skin markings; the underlying cause is often atopic dermatitis, stress, or an untreated infection like candida.

![[page614_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Vulval Psoriasis by the well-demarcated, bright red plaques found on the labia and in the inguinal folds.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** This is a form of "inverse psoriasis." Note the absence of the classic thick, silvery scale usually seen on knees/elbows; the moisture and friction in the genital folds macerate the scale, leaving a smooth, glazed, and sharply defined ("knife-cut") border.

![[page616_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize early Vulval Lichen Sclerosus (LS) by the distinct "ivory white" or porcelain-colored sclerotic plaques.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** LS is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory condition that causes epidermal atrophy (thinning). The "white" appearance is due to sclerosis of the dermis and loss of pigment; students should look for the classic "figure-of-eight" distribution involving the vulva and perianal area.

![[page618_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify advanced Lichen Sclerosus characterized by severe architectural changes and ecchymosis (bruising).**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** Chronic inflammation leads to scarring that fuses the labia minora to the majora and buries the clitoris (clitoral phimosis). The ecchymosis (purpura) occurs because the thinned, atrophic skin has fragile blood vessels that rupture easily; this bruising is a hallmark diagnostic clue for LS.

![[page619_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Penile Lichen Sclerosus, historically referred to as Balanitis Xerotica Obliterans (BXO).**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** Similar to vulval LS, this presents as whitish, scar-like tissue on the glans and prepuce. It is a major cause of phimosis (inability to retract the foreskin) in men and can lead to urethral strictures if the scarring involves the meatus.

![[page621_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish Erosive Vulval Lichen Planus (LP) by the presence of painful, glazed, red erosions near the vestibule and introitus.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** Unlike the "dry and white" appearance of Lichen Sclerosus, Lichen Planus is often "wet and erosive." Look for Wickham’s striae (a lacy white network) at the borders of the redness; this condition involves T-cell attack on basal keratinocytes and can cause vaginal scarring (stenosis).

![[page622_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Erosive Lichen Planus of the penis, presenting as annular or erosive patches on the glans.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** This condition is more common in uncircumcised males. It is crucial to differentiate this from Zoon’s balanitis (plasma cell infiltration) or candidiasis; a violaceous (purple) hue at the margin is a classic hint for Lichen Planus.

![[page626_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Vulval Melanosis as irregular, flat, pigmented macules on the mucosa.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** These are benign mucosal lentigines (freckles) resulting from increased melanin production, not an increase in melanocytes. While benign, the irregular borders make it a "mimicker" of melanoma, so students must know that dermatoscopy or biopsy is often required to rule out malignancy.

![[page629_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Vulval Intra‐epithelial Neoplasia (VIN) by the presence of raised, white (leukoplakia), hyperkeratotic plaques.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** VIN is a pre-cancerous condition. It is categorized into uVIN (Usual type, associated with high-risk HPV infection, common in younger women) and dVIN (Differentiated type, associated with Lichen Sclerosus, common in older women and carries a higher risk of progressing to Squamous Cell Carcinoma).

![[page630_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Perianal Intra‐epithelial Neoplasia (AIN/PAIN), which often presents as brownish or reddish plaques similar to Bowen’s disease.**
    *   **Physiology & Tip:** This is the anal equivalent of VIN and CIN (cervical), driven almost exclusively by high-risk HPV (strains 16 and 18). It is most prevalent in immunosuppressed patients (e.g., HIV+); surveillance is critical as it is the precursor to anal squamous cell carcinoma.
