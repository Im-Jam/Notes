Here is a comprehensive study guide based on the image collection from Chapter 02 on Psoriasis.

![[page083_img01.jpeg]]
* **Understand the T-Cell mediated immune cascade.**
   Psoriasis is not just a skin condition; it is a chronic, systemic inflammatory disease driven by the immune system. Specifically, Th1 and Th17 cells release cytokines (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23) that trigger keratinocytes to reproduce too quickly.
* **Note the "Feed-Forward" loop.**
   The diagram likely illustrates how activated keratinocytes release their own chemokines, calling in *more* immune cells, which creates a self-sustaining cycle of inflammation and skin thickening.

![[page083_img02.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the histological hallmarks: Acanthosis and Parakeratosis.**
   *Acanthosis* refers to the thickening of the epidermal layer, while *parakeratosis* is the retention of nuclei in the stratum corneum (the top layer). This happens because the skin cells are moving to the surface in 3-5 days instead of the normal 28, not having time to mature properly.
* **Identify the cause of the "Auspitz Sign".**
   Look for the elongated "rete ridges" (epidermal extensions dipping down) and dilated capillaries high up in the dermis. If you pick a scale off, you expose these shallow vessels, causing pinpoint bleeding (Auspitz sign).

![[page085_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish between Matrix and Nail Bed defects.**
   (a) Pitting is caused by a defect in the *nail matrix* (the root), resulting in spotty loss of keratin on the surface. Onycholysis is the separation of the nail from the *nail bed*.
* **Look for the "Oil Drop" sign.**
   (b) Hyperkeratosis (thickening) often involves subungual debris. Students should look for yellowish-brown discoloration (salmon patches or oil drops) which is pathognomonic for psoriatic nail disease.

![[page086_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify "Psoriasis Vulgaris" morphology.**
   These small plaques exhibit the classic triad: Erythema (redness), Induration (thickness), and Desquamation (silvery scale).
* **Observe the demarcation.**
   Unlike eczema, which often has fuzzy borders, psoriatic plaques are usually "well-demarcated," meaning you can draw a sharp line with a pen where the lesion starts and stops.

![[page087_img01.jpeg]]
* **Understanding Chronic Static Plaque Psoriasis.**
   These large plaques are often persistent and resistant to treatment. They represent the most common form of the disease.
* **Assess the scale quality.**
   The scale is described as "micaceous" (silver-white and flaky, like mica). This accumulation is due to the lack of normal shedding (desquamation) mechanisms.

![[page089_img01.jpeg]]
* **Memorize the "Extensor" distribution.**
   Psoriasis favors the extensor surfaces (elbows, knees) and the scalp and sacrum. This is a key differentiator from Atopic Dermatitis, which favors flexural surfaces (inner elbows, behind knees) in adults.
* **Note the symmetry.**
   Psoriasis is predominantly bilateral and symmetrical. If a patient has a plaque on the left elbow, check the right elbow.

![[page090_img01.jpeg]]
* **Assessing severity via BSA (Body Surface Area).**
   Generalized plaques indicate a high PASI (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index) score. When coverage is this extensive, topical creams are usually insufficient, and systemic therapy or phototherapy is indicated.
* **Monitor for comorbidities.**
   Patients with generalized inflammation are at higher risk for metabolic syndrome (obesity, hypertension, diabetes) and cardiovascular events due to the systemic inflammatory burden.

![[page091_img01.jpeg]]
* **Differentiate from Tinea Corporis.**
   Truncal plaques can look like ringworm (fungal). However, psoriasis scale is usually uniform across the plaque, whereas tinea has a clear center with a scaling *edge*.
* **Check for "Koebnerization" from clothing.**
   Tight waistbands or bra straps can cause trauma that elicits new plaques on the trunk (Koebner phenomenon).

![[page092_img01.jpeg]]
* **Understand Annular morphology.**
   "Annular" means ring-shaped. This occurs when a plaque begins to heal from the center outwards, leaving a ring of activity.
* **Do not confuse with Erythema Migrans.**
   While ring-shaped, the presence of heavy scale distinguishes this from the target lesion of Lyme disease or other annular erythemas.

![[page093_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define the Koebner Phenomenon (Isomorphic Response).**
   This image shows psoriasis arising in a surgical scar. This demonstrates that trauma (cuts, burns, scratches) can trigger the psoriatic cascade in genetically susceptible skin.
* **Clinical Tip:**
   Advise patients to avoid scrubbing or picking at their skin, as this micro-trauma can generate new lesions.

![[page094_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Hyperkeratotic Palmar Psoriasis.**
   This form often lacks the silvery scale and instead presents as thick, yellow, callous-like skin with painful fissures (cracks).
* **Quality of Life impact.**
   This specific presentation is physically debilitating, making simple tasks like gripping objects or walking (if plantar) extremely painful.

![[page095_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Scalp Psoriasis characteristics.**
   Look for thickening that extends *just beyond* the hairline (the "corona psoriatica"). The scale is often very thick and adherent ("asbestos-like").
* **Hair growth preservation.**
   Unlike fungal infections or discoid lupus, psoriasis is generally non-scarring and does not cause permanent hair loss, though hair may thin temporarily due to the scratching.

![[page096_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Onycholysis accurately.**
   This shows distal separation of the nail plate. Psoriasis is the most common cause of onycholysis.
* **Differential Diagnosis:**
   Always clip the nail for mycology (fungal culture) because psoriatic nails are prone to secondary fungal infections, and the two conditions look very similar.

![[page097_img01.jpeg]]
* **Connect Guttate Psoriasis to Streptococcal infection.**
   "Guttate" comes from the Latin *gutta* (drop). These small, raindrop-like lesions often appear abruptly 2-3 weeks after a Step throat infection (Streptococcal pharyngitis).
* **Prognosis differs from Plaque Psoriasis.**
   This form is most common in children and young adults. It has a better chance of spontaneous remission than chronic plaque psoriasis, though it can evolve into the chronic form.

![[page098_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Palmoplantar Pustulosis.**
   These are sterile pustules (containing neutrophils, not bacteria) on the palms and soles. They dry up to form brown macules.
* **Strong association with Smoking.**
   This specific variant of psoriasis is extremely strongly linked to smoking. Smoking cessation is a primary treatment intervention.

![[page099_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize a Medical Emergency: Generalized Pustular Psoriasis.**
   Also known as *Von Zumbusch* psoriasis. The skin is bright red and covered in sheets of pustules.
* **Understand the trigger and risk.**
   This is often triggered by the sudden withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids ("steroid rebound"). The patient loses skin barrier function, leading to electrolyte imbalance, temperature dysregulation, and sepsis risk.

![[page099_img02.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose "Inverse Psoriasis" (Flexural).**
   Found in armpits, groin, and under breasts. Note the **absence of scale**. It appears shiny, smooth, and beefy red.
* **Why no scale?**
   The moisture and friction in skin folds macerate the scale, so it doesn't build up. Do not misdiagnose as simple chafing or fungal infection (though fungal co-infection is common).

![[page100_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish Napkin Psoriasis from Diaper Dermatitis.**
   Irritant diaper dermatitis usually spares the deep skin folds (creases). Napkin psoriasis *involves* the folds and has a sharper, brighter red border.
* **Look for dissemination.**
   Check the face or other body parts; "napkin" psoriasis can sometimes be the first sign of widespread psoriasis in an infant.

![[page101_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Erythrodermic Psoriasis.**
   Defined as erythema covering >90% of the body surface area. The skin is failing as an organ.
* **Physiological complications.**
   The massive blood flow to the skin can cause high-output cardiac failure. Heat loss leads to hypothermia/chills. Fluid loss leads to dehydration. This requires hospitalization.

![[page103_img01.jpeg]]
* **Screen for Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA).**
   Up to 30% of psoriasis patients develop arthritis. It is "Seronegative" (Rheumatoid Factor negative).
* **Look for "Sausage Digits" (Dactylitis).**
   This image shows swelling of the entire finger, not just the joint. This is a hallmark of PsA, along with asymmetrical joint involvement.

![[page104_img01.jpeg]]
* **Memorize the "Pencil-in-Cup" deformity.**
   This is the classic X-ray finding in severe PsA. The distal bone end is whittled down (pencil) and the adjacent bone surface becomes cupped.
* **Pathophysiology of bone changes.**
   PsA is unique because it causes both bone *erosion* (destruction) and new bone *formation* (proliferation/whiskering) simultaneously.
