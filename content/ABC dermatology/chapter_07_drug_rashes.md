* **Identify the distribution pattern: note that the rash is strictly limited to sun-exposed areas like the face, V of the neck, and arms.** This phenomenon helps distinguish drug-induced photosensitivity from general allergic reactions; areas shielded by clothing or anatomy (like the area under the chin or behind the ears) are typically spared (the "Wilkinson's triangle").
* **Distinguish between Phototoxic (common) and Photoallergic (rare) reactions.** Phototoxic reactions occur due to direct tissue damage (non-immunologic) resembling an exaggerated sunburn and can happen to anyone taking the drug (e.g., tetracyclines). Photoallergic reactions are cell-mediated immune responses (Type IV hypersensitivity) causing an eczematous, itchy rash that appears 24-48 hours after exposure.

![[page209_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the specific hyperpigmentation associated with Diltiazem (a Calcium Channel Blocker).** This usually presents as a reticulated (net-like), slate-grey or blue-grey discoloration on sun-exposed skin.
* **Understand the physiological mechanism involves dermal deposition.** The drug or its metabolites deposit in the dermis and stimulate melanin production; because the pigment is deep in the dermis (Tyndall effect), it appears grey/blue rather than brown.

![[page213_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the "Morbilliform" (measles-like) presentation, which is the most common type of drug eruption.** It consists of diffuse, symmetric erythematous macules and papules that often start on the trunk and spread to the extremities.
* **Note the timing and mechanism (Type IV Hypersensitivity).** This reaction typically appears 7–14 days after starting a new medication (delayed T-cell mediated response). It is often distinct from hives because these lesions are fixed (they do not move around) and usually resolve with desquamation (peeling).

![[page214_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the classic wheals of Urticaria (Hives) characterized by central swelling and surrounding erythema.** These lesions are intensely pruritic (itchy) and transient, meaning individual spots appear and disappear within 24 hours.
* **Understand the Type I Hypersensitivity mechanism.** This is an IgE-mediated immediate reaction causing mast cell degranulation and histamine release. Penicillin is a classic culprit; unlike the maculopapular rash, this carries a risk of anaphylaxis (airway swelling, hypotension).

![[page215_img01.jpeg]]
* **Look for the "Butterfly rash" (Malar rash) similar to Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).** Drug-induced lupus (DILE) presents with skin findings and joint pain but rarely affects the kidneys or CNS compared to idiopathic SLE.
* **Recall the specific serology and resolution.** DILE is associated with **Anti-histone antibodies**. The key clinical feature is that the condition usually resolves completely once the offending drug (classically hydralazine, procainamide, or minocycline) is discontinued.

![[page216_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify "Palpable Purpura," the hallmark of cutaneous vasculitis.** Unlike a bruise or simple rash, you can feel these spots because there is inflammation and fibrin deposition within the small blood vessel walls.
* **Note the gravity-dependent distribution.** These lesions predominantly appear on the lower legs due to hydrostatic pressure. The mechanism is Type III hypersensitivity (immune complex deposition) leading to vessel wall damage and leakage of red blood cells.

![[page217_img01.jpeg]]
* **Observe the Lichenoid reaction mimicking Lichen Planus (purple, polygonal, pruritic papules).** Drug-induced lichenoid eruptions often look exactly like idiopathic Lichen Planus but may lack the classic white lacy lines (Wickham striae) and are slower to resolve.
* **Utilize the "Swollen Ankles" as a diagnostic clue.** Image (b) shows pedal edema, a known side effect of Calcium Channel Blockers (like Nifedipine). If you see a lichenoid rash + swollen ankles, think of a cardiovascular drug as the cause.

![[page219_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the Fixed Drug Eruption by its tendency to recur in the exact same location.** The lesions are typically well-demarcated, round or oval patches that may blister and leave residual hyperpigmentation (dark spots) after healing.
* **Check distinctive sites.** While they can appear anywhere, they favor the lips, genitalia, and hands. The "memory T-cells" residing in that specific patch of skin reactivate every time the drug (commonly NSAIDs or Sulfonamides) is taken.

![[page220_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) as a dermatologic emergency.** The image shows extensive skin detachment and raw dermis, resembling a massive burn.
* **Check for Nikolsky Sign and Mucosal Involvement.** The "Nikolsky sign" is positive if rubbing the skin causes the top layer to slide off. Involvement of mucous membranes (eyes, mouth, genitals) is almost universal in TEN/SJS and distinguishes it from Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome.

![[page222_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify DRESS syndrome (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms).** The facial edema (swollen ears and face) shown in the image is a major clinical clue that distinguishes this from a simple drug rash.
* **Remember the systemic and delayed nature.** DRESS has a long latency period (2–6 weeks after drug start). It is life-threatening because it involves internal organs (liver inflammation, lymphadenopathy) and hematologic abnormalities (high Eosinophils).

![[page223_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis (AGEP) by the "sheet of pustules."** The rash consists of dozens of tiny, sterile (non-infectious) pustules on top of widespread red skin, often starting in body folds (flexural areas).
* **Differentiate from Acne and Infection.** Unlike acne, these pustules are non-follicular (not centered on a hair follicle). Unlike an infection, the pustules are sterile. The reaction occurs rapidly (within 48 hours of drug intake, often antibiotics) and is accompanied by high fever and high neutrophil count.
