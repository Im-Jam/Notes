* **The hair follicle undergoes a continuous life cycle consisting of three main phases: Anagen (growth), Catagen (involution), and Telogen (resting).**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** Anagen is the active growth phase which determines hair length (lasts 2–6 years). Catagen is a brief transition (2–3 weeks) where the follicle shrinks. Telogen is the resting phase (3 months) which concludes with Exogen (shedding).
    *   **Study Tip:** In a healthy scalp, roughly 85–90% of hairs are in Anagen. If a patient experiences sudden, diffuse shedding, it is often "Telogen Effluvium," where a stressor pushes too many Anagen hairs into Telogen simultaneously.

![[page550_img01.jpeg]]
* **The Norwood scale classifies Male Pattern Hair Loss (Androgenetic Alopecia), progressing from temporal recession to vertex thinning, and finally to a horseshoe pattern.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This condition is driven by Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) acting on genetically susceptible follicles. DHT shortens the Anagen phase, leading to "follicular miniaturization" (hairs become shorter and thinner/vellus-like).
    *   **Simplification:** Notice that the occipital scalp (back of the head) is usually spared. This is why hair transplant donor sites are taken from the back of the head—those follicles are resistant to DHT.

![[page552_img01.jpeg]]
* **The Ludwig scale classifies Female Pattern Hair Loss (FPHL), characterized by diffuse thinning over the crown with preservation of the frontal hairline.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** Unlike men, women rarely go completely bald. The pathology is still follicular miniaturization, but the pattern is a centrifugal expansion of thinning from the central part.
    *   **Extra Tip:** When examining, look for the "widened part." If the part width is significantly wider at the top of the head compared to the back (occiput), it suggests FPHL.

![[page553_img01.jpeg]]
* **This illustrates the "Christmas Tree" pattern of female hair loss, where the thinning is widest at the frontal hairline and narrows toward the vertex.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is a variation of the Ludwig pattern. It highlights the "frontal accentuation" of the hair loss.
    *   **Simplification:** Use this pattern to differentiate genetic loss from Telogen Effluvium. Telogen Effluvium causes diffuse shedding all over the head, whereas FPHL is concentrated on the top/front.

![[page555_img01.jpeg]]
* **Alopecia Areata presents as well-demarcated, smooth, round or oval patches of complete hair loss.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is an autoimmune disease where T-lymphocytes attack the hair bulb, forcing the follicle into premature dormancy. It is **non-scarring**, meaning the follicle remains intact and regrowth is possible.
    *   **Extra Tip:** Check the skin within the patch. In Alopecia Areata, the skin is smooth and normal (no scale, no redness). If there is scale, consider fungal infection (Tinea Capitis).

![[page556_img01.jpeg]]
* **"Exclamation mark hairs" are the hallmark diagnostic sign of active Alopecia Areata.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** These are short broken hairs that taper toward the scalp (narrower at the base than the tip). They occur because the immune attack weakens the hair shaft as it is being formed, causing it to break when it reaches the surface.
    *   **Study Tip:** You will find these at the *periphery* (edges) of an expanding patch. Their presence indicates the disease is currently active and the patch is likely to grow.

![[page557_img01.jpeg]]
* **Nail changes, specifically fine geometric pitting (trachyonychia), are a common extracutaneous finding in Alopecia Areata.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** The same inflammatory cells attacking the hair follicle can attack the nail matrix, causing defective keratinization of the nail plate.
    *   **Simplification:** Always check the nails in hair loss patients. If you see pitting, it strongly supports an autoimmune diagnosis (like Alopecia Areata or Psoriasis) rather than a fungal or hormonal one.

![[page560_img01.jpeg]]
* **Tinea Capitis (scalp ringworm) typically presents as patches of alopecia with scaling and broken hairs (often called "black dots").**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is a dermatophyte (fungal) infection that invades the hair shaft. "Ectothrix" infection coats the outside of the hair; "Endothrix" invades the inside, making the hair brittle so it breaks at the scalp surface (black dot).
    *   **Extra Tip:** It is most common in children. Unlike Alopecia Areata (smooth skin), Tinea Capitis almost always has scale, crust, or erythema.

![[page561_img01.jpeg]]
* **A Kerion is a severe, boggy, weeping, and tender inflammatory mass caused by an intense immune reaction to a fungal infection (Tinea Capitis).**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This represents a hypersensitivity reaction to the fungus. The inflammation is so severe it can destroy follicles.
    *   **Important Clinical Point:** A Kerion is a medical urgency in dermatology. Immediate oral antifungals and sometimes steroids are needed to prevent permanent **scarring alopecia**.

![[page563_img01.jpeg]]
* **Discoid Lupus Erythematosus (DLE) of the scalp causes scarring alopecia characterized by erythema, scaling, and follicular plugging.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is a lymphocytic primary scarring alopecia. The inflammation targets the bulge region (stem cells), leading to permanent loss.
    *   **Extra Tip:** Look for the "carpet tack" sign: if you peel off a scale, you may see spikes of keratin on the underside that were plugged into the follicles. The center of the patch becomes white and atrophic (scarred).

![[page565_img01.jpeg]]
* **Lichen Planopilaris (LPP) presents with perifollicular erythema (redness around the hair) and follicular hyperkeratosis (scale around the hair).**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is a variant of Lichen Planus affecting the scalp. It destroys the follicle resulting in smooth, shiny patches of scarring alopecia.
    *   **Simplification:** Look for the "lonely hair" sign. Unlike DLE which is a large plaque, LPP often affects individual follicles at the edges of the patch. The center is smooth/scarred, while the active edge has redness and scaling around remaining hairs.

![[page566_img01.jpeg]]
* **Pseudopelade of Brocq is a pattern of scarring alopecia appearing as small, smooth, flesh-colored patches resembling "footprints in the snow."**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** It is often considered the "burn-out" or end-stage of other scarring alopecias (like LPP) rather than a specific disease itself.
    *   **Study Tip:** "Brocq" sounds like "Broken"—think of broken, scattered patches. Unlike DLE or active LPP, there is very little inflammation (no redness or scale) visible; the hair is just gone, and the skin is atrophic.

![[page567_img01.jpeg]]
* **Folliculitis Decalvans is a neutrophilic scarring alopecia characterized by "tufted folliculitis" (multiple hairs emerging from one pore) and pustules.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This is a chronic inflammation often associated with *Staphylococcus aureus*. The scarring process fuses multiple follicular openings together, creating the "doll's hair" or tufted appearance.
    *   **Simplification:** If you see pus, crusting, and tufts of hair, think bacterial involvement (neutrophils). Treatment requires antibiotics, unlike the autoimmune scarring alopecias.

![[page569_img01.jpeg]]
* **Hirsutism is excessive terminal hair growth in women in androgen-dependent areas (e.g., beard, chest, inner thighs).**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** This indicates either an excess of androgens (like testosterone in PCOS) or increased sensitivity of the hair follicle to normal androgen levels. It transforms fine vellus hair into coarse terminal hair.
    *   **Differentiation:** Only women can have hirsutism. If a man has a hairy chest, it's normal; if a woman does, it's hirsutism.

![[page572_img01.jpeg]]
* **Hypertrichosis is excessive hair growth (density or length) that is independent of androgens and can occur anywhere on the body.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** It can be congenital (werewolf syndrome) or acquired. Common acquired causes include malnutrition (Anorexia Nervosa—lanugo hair) or drugs (Minoxidil, Cyclosporine, Phenytoin).
    *   **Simplification:** Differentiate from Hirsutism: Hypertrichosis is "hair where hair normally grows, just too much of it," or hair in non-sexual areas (like the forehead). It affects both men and women.

![[page574_img01.jpeg]]
* **Scalp Psoriasis presents as well-demarcated erythematous plaques with thick, silvery-white scale, often extending past the hairline.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** Psoriasis is a disorder of keratinocyte hyperproliferation. On the scalp, it creates thick plaques.
    *   **Extra Tip:** Psoriasis is typically **non-alopecic** (doesn't cause hair loss). However, the thick scales can bind hair, and removal of the scale can mechanically pull hair out. Look for plaques behind the ears and at the occiput.

![[page575_img01.jpeg]]
* **Pityriasis Amiantacea is a reaction pattern where thick, asbestos-like scales bind proximal hair shafts together in bundles.**
    *   **Physiological Insight:** It is not a specific diagnosis but a presentation of conditions like psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, or lichen simplex. The scale adheres so tightly to the hair that it grows out with the hair shaft.
    *   **Simplification:** If you see "matted" hair with thick, sticky scale that looks like overlaps of roof tiles or asbestos, this is the term to use. It often leads to temporary hair loss due to the mechanical stress on the hair.
