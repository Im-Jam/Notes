Here is the modified study material designed to orient students to the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and key distinguishing features of acne, rosacea, and hidradenitis suppurativa.

![[page351_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the components of the pilosebaceous unit.** This histological view shows the hair follicle, the hair shaft, and the sebaceous gland, which are the "stage" where acne occurs.
* **Understand the four key pathogenic factors of acne.** (1) Follicular hyperkeratinization (plugging), (2) Excess sebum production, (3) *Cutibacterium acnes* proliferation, and (4) Inflammation.
* **Note the microcomedone formation.** In the pathology slide, look for the widening of the follicle and the dense keratin plug blocking the opening; this is the invisible precursor to all visible acne lesions.

![[page352_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Closed Comedones (Whiteheads).** These present as flesh-colored or white, non-inflamed papules where the follicular ostium (opening) is obstructed by keratin and sebum.
* **Physiological distinction:** Because the opening is "closed" or very narrow, the contents are not exposed to air, preventing oxidation; this keeps the material white rather than turning black.

![[page354_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Open Comedones (Blackheads).** These are dilated follicles impacted with keratin and lipids; the black color is due to the oxidation of melanin and surface lipids upon exposure to air, **not** dirt.
* **Identify Nodulocystic changes.** The image shows cysts (deep, fluctuant nodules) and scarring; cysts occur when the follicle wall ruptures deep in the dermis, triggering a massive inflammatory response and increasing the risk of permanent fibrosis (scarring).

![[page357_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Perioral Dermatitis.** Look for clusters of inflammatory papules and pustules around the mouth, often with a scaling base.
* **Crucial diagnostic tip (The "Spare Tire" sign):** Note the narrow zone of normal skin immediately adjacent to the vermilion border of the lips; this sparing distinguishes perioral dermatitis from other eczematous conditions or contact cheilitis. Avoid topical steroids here, as they exacerbate the condition.

![[page358_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Inflammatory Acne.** This presents with erythematous papules (red bumps) and pustules (pus-filled bumps).
* **Pathophysiology of the "Zit":** This occurs when *C. acnes* bacteria incite an immune response, drawing neutrophils into the follicle; the white center is a collection of neutrophils and necrotic debris.

![[page359_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Acne Keloidalis Nuchae.** This is a chronic folliculitis found on the occipital scalp/neck, primarily affecting men of African descent.
* **Mechanism:** It begins as pustules and evolves into firm, keloidal papules and plaques; mechanical irritation (like stiff collars) and curved hair shafts re-entering the skin (ingrown hairs) perpetuate the inflammation and scarring.

![[page361_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify "Ice-pick" Scars.** These are deep, narrow, sharp-edged depressions that extend deep into the dermis, resembling a hole made by an ice pick.
* **Treatment implication:** Because these scars are deep rather than wide, they often do not respond well to surface resurfacing (like dermabrasion) alone and may require punch excision or chemical reconstruction (TCA cross).

![[page362_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Keloid Scaring.** Unlike atrophic (depressed) scars, these are hypertrophic, raised, firm lesions where collagen production has gone unchecked.
* **Key characteristic:** A true keloid extends *beyond* the boundaries of the original acne lesion/wound, whereas a hypertrophic scar stays within the margins. This is most common on the jawline, chest, and back.

![[page363_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Acne Excoriée (Picker's Acne).** Look for excoriations, crusts, and superficial erosions that are linear or geometric, often without primary comedones.
* **Psychological component:** This is often driven by a compulsive desire to pick at perceived imperfections; students should recognize this requires a gentle, supportive approach and potentially psychodermatology management, not just acne creams.

![[page364_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Acne Conglobata.** This is a severe, chronic form of nodulocystic acne characterized by burrowing and interconnecting abscesses and sinus tracts.
* **Follicular Occlusion Tetrad:** Acne conglobata is often grouped with Hidradenitis Suppurativa, dissecting cellulitis of the scalp, and pilonidal sinus; all share the mechanism of follicular occlusion and severe inflammation.

![[page365_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Acne Fulminans.** Identify the sudden, explosive onset of ulcerative, necrotic acne lesions (usually on the chest and back) accompanied by systemic symptoms.
* **Systemic signs to check:** Patients will often have fever, leukocytosis (high white blood cell count), and polyarthralgia (joint pain). This is a dermatologic emergency requiring systemic steroids and eventually isotretinoin.

![[page369_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Cheilitis (Dry Lips) secondary to Isotretinoin.** This shows dry, peeling, and cracked lips, which is the most predictable side effect of oral isotretinoin therapy.
* **Dosing indicator:** This is dose-dependent; if a student observes a patient on isotretinoin *without* dry lips, the patient is likely not taking the medication or the dose is too low (poor absorption).

![[page370_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Retinoid Dermatitis.** Note the dry, erythematous, eczematous patches on the dorsal hands and forearms.
* **Physiology:** Isotretinoin drastically reduces sebum production and thins the stratum corneum, compromising the skin barrier and making it susceptible to xerosis (dryness) and environmental irritation.

![[page372_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Rosacea (Erythematotelangiectatic type).** Look for persistent central facial erythema (redness) and telangiectasias (visible broken blood vessels).
* **Distinguishing feature:** Unlike acne, rosacea typically **lacks comedones** (no blackheads or whiteheads); the trigger is vascular hyperreactivity (flushing) to stimuli like heat, alcohol, spicy food, and sun.

![[page373_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Papulopustular Rosacea.** This presents with inflammatory papules and pustules on a background of erythema, localized here to the nose.
* **Clinical Tip:** This is often misdiagnosed as "adult acne," but the absence of comedones and the presence of background flushing clues you into rosacea.

![[page373_img02.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Rhinophyma.** This is Phymatous Rosacea, characterized by skin thickening, irregular surface nodules, and enlargement of the nose.
* **Pathology:** It represents massive sebaceous gland hyperplasia and fibrosis; it is far more common in men and usually requires surgical or laser intervention rather than just medical therapy.

![[page374_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Ocular Rosacea (Blepharitis).** Note the inflammation of the eyelid margins; patients may complain of a "gritty" sensation, burning, or styes.
* **Clinical significance:** Ocular symptoms can occur *before* cutaneous (skin) symptoms in up to 20% of patients; untreated ocular rosacea can lead to keratitis and corneal damage.

![[page376_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS).** This image shows Hurley Stage III: diffuse involvement with multiple interconnected sinus tracts, abscesses, and scarring across the entire axilla.
* **Pathophysiology:** This is a disease of the **apocrine** gland-bearing skin (axilla, groin, inframammary); it begins with follicular occlusion, rupture, and secondary inflammation, leading to permanent tunnel formation under the skin.
