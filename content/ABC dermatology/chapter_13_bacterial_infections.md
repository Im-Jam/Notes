* **Identify the hallmark of Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL) toxicity.** This specific strain of *Staphylococcus aureus* produces a cytotoxin that creates pores in the membranes of leukocytes, leading to recurrent, necrotic skin infections.
* **Observe the multiple sites of abscess formation.** Unlike a simple boil, PVL-positive infections often present as multiple, deep-seated, painful abscesses that are resistant to standard treatments and prone to rapid recurrence.
* **Physiological Note:** The PVL toxin causes lysis of white blood cells (neutrophils), which releases inflammatory mediators that cause significant tissue necrosis (tissue death). This is why these lesions are often more aggressive and inflammatory than standard staph infections.

![[page384_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the classic "honey-colored crust."** This is the pathognomonic sign for non-bullous impetigo, the most common bacterial skin infection in children.
* **Understand the composition of the crust.** The golden color results from the drying of serous exudate (serum) mixed with bacteria and cellular debris after the pustule breaks.
* **Simplification:** Think of this as a superficial infection of the epidermis. Because it is superficial, it typically heals without scarring. The primary pathogens are usually *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Streptococcus pyogenes*.

![[page385_img01.jpeg]]
* **Differentiate Bullous Impetigo from the non-bullous form.** Note the presence of flaccid bullae (large blisters) and shallow erosions where blisters have ruptured.
* **Identify the toxin mechanism involved.** This presentation is caused specifically by *Staphylococcus aureus* strains that produce exfoliative toxins.
* **Physiological Note:** The exfoliative toxin targets **desmoglein 1**, a protein responsible for holding skin cells together in the superficial epidermis. When this protein is cleaved, the skin cells separate, causing the blister.

![[page386_img01.jpeg]]
* **Locate the inflammation relative to the hair shaft.** Bacterial folliculitis presents as a pustule or erythematous papule that is pierced centrally by a hair.
* **Assess the depth of infection.** This is a superficial infection of the upper portion of the hair follicle, usually caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*.
* **Extra Tip:** If the infection goes deeper, it becomes a furuncle (boil); if multiple furuncles coalesce, it becomes a carbuncle.

![[page387_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish this from true bacterial infection.** "Pseudo" folliculitis is a foreign body inflammatory reaction, not a primary bacterial infection.
* **Understand the mechanical cause.** This occurs when a hair curves back and re-enters the skin (ingrown hair), causing inflammation.
* **Simplification:** While often seen in the beard area (*pseudofolliculitis barbae*), this image shows it can occur anywhere shaved or subjected to friction, like the forehead. The primary treatment involves stopping the hair removal method that causes the ingrowth.

![[page388_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the specific location and morphology.** Note the firm, papular, or keloid-like plaques located on the nape of the neck (nuchal area).
* **Understand the chronic nature of this condition.** This is a chronic, scarring folliculitis that leads to fibrosis (scarring) and hair loss (alopecia).
* **Extra Tip:** Despite the name "acne," this is not related to common acne vulgaris. It is a structural issue often exacerbated by friction (collars) and short haircuts, leading to a cycle of inflammation and hypertrophic scarring.

![[page389_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the location and appearance.** Erythrasma presents as reddish-brown, slightly scaly patches in intertriginous areas (skin folds like the axilla or groin).
* **Identify the causative organism.** This is caused by *Corynebacterium minutissimum*, a gram-positive rod.
* **Diagnostic Tip:** Under a **Wood's lamp** (UV light), these lesions fluoresce a brilliant **coral-red**. This is due to the bacteria producing porphyrins. This distinguishes it from fungal infections (tinea) which do not fluoresce red.

![[page390_img01.jpeg]]
* **Note the sharp demarcation of the lesion.** Erysipelas is characterized by a bright red, raised plaque with a very clearly defined border separating it from normal skin.
* **Understand the anatomical depth.** It involves the upper dermis and superficial lymphatics.
* **Physiological Note:** The "step-off" or raised edge is due to the intense edema in the superficial dermis. The most common pathogen is Group A *Streptococcus*.

![[page391_img01.jpeg]]
* **Contrast this with Erysipelas.** Cellulitis involves the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue, leading to diffuse redness with **ill-defined borders** (you cannot easily draw a line where the infection stops).
* **Look for portals of entry.** Cellulitis often originates from a break in the skin barrier, such as a wound, ulcer, or even tinea pedis (athlete's foot) between the toes.
* **Simplification:** If the edge is blurry and the redness is deep, think Cellulitis. If the edge is sharp and raised, think Erysipelas.

![[page392_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the systemic nature of this syndrome.** SSSS presents with widespread erythema and superficial peeling of the skin, resembling a burn or scald.
* **Understand the hematogenous spread.** Unlike Bullous Impetigo (where the toxin is local), here the *Staph* exfoliative toxin spreads through the blood, affecting the skin distantly.
* **Extra Tip:** Look for the **Nikolsky sign**, where gentle pressure on healthy-looking skin causes the top layer to shear off. SSSS typically spares the mucous membranes (unlike drug reactions like Stevens-Johnson Syndrome).

![[page393_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the "punched-out" ulceration.** Ecthyma is essentially a deep form of impetigo that extends through the epidermis into the dermis.
* **Note the hard crust.** It is covered by a thick, adherent crust (eschar) with a red margin.
* **Physiological Note:** Because it penetrates the dermis, ecthyma will heal with **scarring**, unlike superficial impetigo. It is often associated with poor hygiene or neglected superficial injuries.

![[page395_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify this as a form of cutaneous Tuberculosis.** Lupus Vulgaris is a progressive, chronic form of TB infection of the skin.
* **Look for the "Apple-Jelly" sign.** When a glass slide is pressed against the lesion (diascopy) to blanch the blood, the granulomatous nodules appear yellowish-brown, resembling apple jelly.
* **Simplification:** This is not related to Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE). The term "Lupus" (wolf) was used historically to describe the destructive, eroding nature of the disease if left untreated.

![[page397_img01.jpeg]]
* **Locate the lesions on the posterior calves.** Erythema Induratum presents as tender, erythematous nodules on the back of the lower legs.
* **Understand the mechanism.** This is a **panniculitis** (inflammation of subcutaneous fat) and is considered a tuberculid—a hypersensitivity reaction to *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* elsewhere in the body.
* **Extra Tip:** It tends to ulcerate and drain, distinguishing it from Erythema Nodosum (which is usually on the shins/anterior and does not ulcerate).

![[page399_img01.jpeg]]
* **Trace the path of the infection.** Note the linear arrangement of nodules ascending up the arm.
* **Define "Sporotrichoid spread."** This pattern follows the lymphatic drainage channels. While classically associated with the fungus *Sporothrix*, this image specifically shows *Mycobacterium marinum*.
* **Clinical History Tip:** This is known as "Fish Tank Granuloma." Always ask about exposure to aquariums, fish, or swimming pools if you see this pattern.

![[page400_img01.jpeg]]
* **Describe the vascular appearance.** The lesions are bright red to purple, berry-like papules that bleed easily.
* **Identify the patient population.** Bacillary Angiomatosis is caused by *Bartonella* species (cat scratch disease bacteria) but specifically manifests this way in **immunocompromised patients (e.g., HIV/AIDS)**.
* **Physiological Note:** The bacteria induce **vascular proliferation** (growth of new blood vessels), which is why the lesions look like angiomas (collections of blood vessels).

![[page402_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the petechial rash.** The rash consists of small, non-blanching red spots caused by bleeding under the skin.
* **Note the direction of spread.** Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF) rash is **centripetal**: it starts on the wrists and ankles (acral) and spreads inward to the trunk, palms, and soles.
* **Extra Tip:** This is a tick-borne illness (*Rickettsia rickettsii*) that causes vasculitis (inflammation of blood vessels). It is a medical emergency.

![[page404_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the "Great Imitator."** Secondary syphilis can present with various rashes, but this image likely shows the classic diffuse, copper-colored papulosquamous rash.
* **Check the palms and soles.** Involvement of the palms and soles is a major diagnostic clue for secondary syphilis.
* **Simplification:** This stage occurs weeks to months after the primary chancre (painless ulcer) has healed. The bacteria (*Treponema pallidum*) have disseminated through the blood. The rash is highly infectious.
