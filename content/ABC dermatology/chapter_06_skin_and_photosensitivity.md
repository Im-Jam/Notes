Here is a comprehensive study guide oriented around the visual presentation of photosensitivity disorders, designed to help you differentiate clinical features and understand the underlying pathophysiology.

![[page188_img01.jpeg]]
* **Understand the electromagnetic spectrum and how different wavelengths penetrate the skin.** Shorter wavelengths (UVB) cause direct DNA damage and sunburn, while longer wavelengths (UVA) penetrate deeper into the dermis causing aging.
    *   **Physiological Point:** UVB (290–320 nm) is responsible for the "Burning" and acts primarily on the epidermis. UVA (320–400 nm) is responsible for "Aging" and photo-allergies; it penetrates through window glass and acts on the dermis.
    *   **Simplification:** Remember the mnemonic: **UV-B** for **B**urning (Epidermis), **UV-A** for **A**ging (Dermis).

![[page193_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Porphyria Cutanea Tarda (PCT) by looking for fragile blisters and erosions on the dorsal hands.** Note the presence of crusting, scarring, and milia (tiny white cysts) in sun-exposed areas.
    *   **Physiological Point:** This is caused by a deficiency in the enzyme uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase, leading to porphyrin buildup in the skin which reacts with light.
    *   **Extra Tip:** PCT is the most common porphyria. Always screen these patients for liver triggers: Alcohol use, Hepatitis C, and Estrogen therapy.
    *   **Diagnostic Clue:** The urine in these patients often turns pink or coral-red under a Wood’s lamp due to excess porphyrins.

![[page194_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Erythropoietic Protoporphyria (EPP) as a condition causing intense pain rather than immediate blistering.** Look for a child presenting with crying or stinging skin after sun exposure, often with minimal visible changes initially.
    *   **Physiological Point:** Unlike PCT, the defect here is in ferrochelatase. Protoporphyrin accumulates in blood vessels and skin, causing immediate phototoxicity upon exposure to visible blue light (not just UV).
    *   **Extra Tip:** Over time, chronic rubbing and damage lead to the waxy, thickened scars seen on the knuckles in this image.
    *   **Simplification:** Think of EPP as "The Screaming Child" syndrome—the pain is subjective and disproportionate to the rash (which appears late).

![[page195_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify a phototoxic drug eruption by its exaggerated "sunburn" appearance.** Observe the sharp demarcation lines where clothing or anatomy protected the skin.
    *   **Physiological Point:** This is a non-immunologic reaction where a drug (e.g., tetracyclines, thiazides) absorbs UV energy and releases it into tissue, causing direct cellular damage.
    *   **Diagnostic Clue:** Look for the **"Submental Sparing"** (the shadow area under the chin and behind the ears) seen in this image. If those areas are clear, it strongly suggests an external light source (the sun) rather than a systemic allergic rash.

![[page198_img01.jpeg]]
* **Diagnose Phytophotodermatitis by the bizarre, streaky, or linear patterns of inflammation.** These irregular shapes correspond to where plant juices have brushed against the skin before sun exposure.
    *   **Physiological Point:** This is a phototoxic reaction caused by **Furocoumarins (Psoralens)** found in plants like limes, celery, and giant hogweed, reacting with UVA light.
    *   **Simplification:** This is often called "Lime Disease" or "Bartender's Dermatitis." It eventually heals with hyperpigmentation (brown staining) that can last for months.

![[page199_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Polymorphous Light Eruption (PMLE) as an itchy, papular rash appearing hours or days after exposure.** This is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction, unlike the immediate burn of phototoxicity.
    *   **Physiological Point:** "Polymorphous" means many shapes—it can be papules, vesicles, or plaques—but within one individual, the rash usually looks the same every time.
    *   **Extra Tip:** Note the "Hardening Phenomenon": symptoms are worst in the spring and early summer but often improve as the skin adapts to sunlight later in the season.
    *   **Simplification:** It is the most common photodermatosis, affecting young women most frequently.

![[page201_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Chronic Actinic Dermatitis (CAD) by the thickened, leathery (lichenified) plaques on sun-exposed sites.** This condition usually affects elderly men and resembles severe, chronic eczema.
    *   **Physiological Point:** This represents a state of persistent light reactivity, often evolving from chronic contact dermatitis or photo-allergy. The skin becomes infiltrated with lymphocytes (pseudolymphoma).
    *   **Extra Tip:** The sensitivity is extreme—patients react to UVB, UVA, and often visible light. The rash often stops sharply at the collar line or shirt cuffs.
