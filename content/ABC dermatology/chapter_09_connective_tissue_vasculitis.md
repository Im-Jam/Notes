Here is a comprehensive study guide covering Connective Tissue Diseases and Vasculitis, oriented for student review based on the image collection.

![[page253_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify "Palpable Purpura" as the hallmark of cutaneous vasculitis.**
    *   *Explanation:* Unlike simple erythema which blanches (turns white) when pressed, purpura does not blanch because red blood cells have leaked out of the vessels. "Palpable" implies there is inflammation and edema in the vessel wall, raising the lesion slightly above the skin surface.
*   **Recognize the gravity dependent distribution.**
    *   *Explanation:* Vasculitis rashes often appear on the lower legs first due to hydrostatic pressure; venous stasis makes it harder for blood to return, exacerbating the leakage from inflamed capillaries.

![[page254_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize necrosis as a sign of small-to-medium vessel occlusion.**
    *   *Explanation:* When inflammation is severe enough to completely block blood flow (ischemia), the tissue dies (necrosis), turning black or forming an ulcer.
*   **Understand the mechanism of blister (bulla) formation in vasculitis.**
    *   *Explanation:* Intense inflammation at the dermal vessels leads to fluid accumulation and death of the overlying epidermis, causing the skin layers to separate and form a blister. This indicates a more severe, acute process than simple purpura.

![[page256_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Livedo Reticularis as a net-like, mottled pattern.**
    *   *Explanation:* This pattern results from the obstruction of the central feeding arteriole in a skin segment; the edges of the segment (the net pattern) are deoxygenated.
*   **Link these findings to Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN).**
    *   *Explanation:* PAN is a systemic vasculitis of medium-sized arteries. Physiologically, it can cause micro-aneurysms ("nodes"). *Tip:* Always check for Hepatitis B status in patients presenting with PAN.

![[page258_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Diagnose Perniosis (Chilblains) based on acral location and history.**
    *   *Explanation:* This is an abnormal inflammatory response to cold (damp, non-freezing temperatures), causing vasoconstriction followed by vasodilation and leakage.
*   **Differentiate from Raynaud's phenomenon.**
    *   *Explanation:* Raynaud's is a transient vasospasm (white-blue-red color change). Perniosis presents as fixed, itchy, painful, red-to-purple inflammatory nodules or plaques, commonly on toes or fingers.

![[page260_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Morphoea as "localized scleroderma".**
    *   *Explanation:* This involves excess collagen deposition causing the skin to feel hard and bound down (indurated).
*   **Look for the "Lilac Ring" of activity.**
    *   *Explanation:* In early/active lesions, you may see a violaceous (purple) border expanding outward, which indicates active inflammation. As the center burns out, it becomes ivory/white and sclerotic. Unlike systemic sclerosis, morphoea rarely affects internal organs.

![[page261_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Calcinosis Cutis as rock-hard, painful nodules.**
    *   *Explanation:* These are deposits of calcium salts in the skin. If they ulcerate, they discharge a chalky white material.
*   **Understand the pathophysiology of Dystrophic Calcification.**
    *   *Explanation:* This occurs despite normal serum calcium levels. It happens because of local tissue damage (from the autoimmune process) acting as a nidus for calcium deposition.

![[page261_img02.jpeg]]
*   **Use the CREST acronym for Limited Systemic Sclerosis.**
    *   *Explanation:* **C**alcinosis, **R**aynaud’s, **E**sophageal dysmotility, **S**clerodactyly, **T**elangiectasia. This form of scleroderma generally has a better prognosis than the diffuse type.
*   **Observe Sclerodactyly (tapering of fingers).**
    *   *Explanation:* Skin tightening results in a shiny appearance, loss of skin creases, and contractures (claw-like hands) due to collagen fibrosis limiting movement.

![[page263_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Memorize the "6 Ps" of Lichen Planus.**
    *   *Explanation:* **P**lanar (flat-topped), **P**urple, **P**olygonal, **P**ruritic, **P**apules, and **P**laques.
*   **Identify Wickham Striae on the surface.**
    *   *Explanation:* Look closely for a fine, white, lace-like network on top of the papules. This is a key diagnostic feature of Lichen Planus.

![[page264_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize the reticular pattern on the buccal mucosa.**
    *   *Explanation:* Lichen Planus frequently affects the mouth. The white lacy pattern inside the cheek is the most common presentation, though it can also be erosive (painful/ulcerated).
*   **Understand the T-cell mediated mechanism.**
    *   *Explanation:* This is an autoimmune attack by cytotoxic T-cells against basal keratinocytes. *Tip:* Oral LP can be chronic and has a very small risk of transforming into squamous cell carcinoma.

![[page265_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Bullous Lichen Planus as a rare variant.**
    *   *Explanation:* The inflammation at the basement membrane zone is so intense that the epidermis separates from the dermis, creating a blister within an existing Lichen Planus plaque.

![[page267_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Link Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) to thrombosis and livedo.**
    *   *Explanation:* Patients have "sticky blood" (hypercoagulability) due to antibodies like Lupus Anticoagulant. This leads to clots in arteries or veins.
*   **Recognize the cutaneous signs of vascular occlusion.**
    *   *Explanation:* In the skin, this manifests as livedo reticularis (mottling), splinter haemorrhages, or frank necrosis (as seen on the ear here) due to blocked blood supply.

![[page268_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the classic Malar "Butterfly" Rash.**
    *   *Explanation:* Erythema over the cheeks and bridge of the nose. Crucially, it typically **spares the nasolabial folds** (the creases by the nose), which helps distinguish it from other facial rashes like rosacea or seborrheic dermatitis.
*   **Understand the role of Photosensitivity.**
    *   *Explanation:* UV light causes keratinocyte apoptosis and DNA damage, which triggers the autoimmune flare. Patients must strictly avoid sun exposure.

![[page270_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Associate annular/polycyclic scaling lesions with Subacute Cutaneous Lupus (SCLE).**
    *   *Explanation:* These lesions look like ringworm (annular) but are autoimmune. They often heal without scarring, unlike Discoid Lupus.
*   **Check for Drug-Induced triggers.**
    *   *Explanation:* SCLE is strongly associated with Anti-Ro (SS-A) antibodies and can be triggered by drugs like Terbinafine (antifungals), PPIs, or thiazide diuretics.

![[page271_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Discoid Lupus Erythematosus (DLE) by its scarring nature.**
    *   *Explanation:* DLE involves deep inflammation that destroys hair follicles (scarring alopecia) and causes skin atrophy (thinning/depression).
*   **Look for the "Carpet Tack" sign.**
    *   *Explanation:* The scale is adherent; if you peel it back, you see spikes of keratin that were plugged into the hair follicles (follicular plugging). The "Conchal bowl" of the ear is a classic location.

![[page272_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Understand the passive transfer of autoimmunity in Neonatal Lupus.**
    *   *Explanation:* This is not "true" lupus in the baby. It is caused by maternal Anti-Ro/La antibodies crossing the placenta.
*   **Recognize the "Raccoon Eyes" and cardiac risks.**
    *   *Explanation:* While the annular rash usually resolves as maternal antibodies clear (approx. 6 months), the most feared complication is **Congenital Heart Block**, which is permanent.

![[page274_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the "V-sign" or "Shawl sign".**
    *   *Explanation:* A photosensitive, macular red rash on the upper chest (V-neck area) or upper back/shoulders (Shawl area).
*   **Link Dermatomyositis to Malignancy.**
    *   *Explanation:* Dermatomyositis in adults is a potential paraneoplastic syndrome. You must screen these patients for underlying cancers (ovarian, lung, GI, etc.).

![[page275_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Diagnose Gottron’s Papules (Pathognomonic).**
    *   *Explanation:* Violaceous (purple/red), flat-topped papules over the **knuckles** (MCP and IP joints).
*   **Differentiate from SLE hands.**
    *   *Explanation:* Lupus rash typically occurs on the skin *between* the knuckles (phalanges), whereas Dermatomyositis occurs *over* the bony prominences of the knuckles.

![[page276_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Examine the nail folds for "Ragged Cuticles" (Samitz sign).**
    *   *Explanation:* Cuticles become overgrown, thickened, and ragged.
*   **Look for nail fold telangiectasia.**
    *   *Explanation:* Using a dermatoscope (or naked eye), you can see dilated, tortuous capillary loops at the nail base. This is a sign of systemic microvascular damage common in Dermatomyositis and Scleroderma.
