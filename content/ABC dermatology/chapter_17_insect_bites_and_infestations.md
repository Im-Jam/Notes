Here is the comprehensive study guide for Chapter 17, oriented for medical students.

![[page490_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the grouping pattern of the erythematous papules.** Note that the lesions are numerous and clustered, indicating multiple feeding events.
    *   **Explanation:** Bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius*) are nocturnal feeders. This severe reaction suggests a heavy infestation. The host immune response is to the injected saliva, which acts as an anticoagulant and anesthetic.
* **Look for central punctums within the weals.** A central hemorrhagic spot is often visible in the center of the hive-like swelling.
    *   **Explanation:** This punctum marks the exact site of the bite. While the wheal (swelling) may fade, the central crust or spot may persist longer.

![[page491_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the "Breakfast, Lunch, and Dinner" sign.** Observe how the three bite reactions are arranged in a linear configuration.
    *   **Explanation:** This linearity is a hallmark of insect bites (especially bed bugs or fleas). It occurs because the insect crawls along the skin or is disturbed during feeding and bites again immediately nearby.
* **Differentiate this from systemic rashes.** Systemic viral or drug exanthems are usually symmetrical and widespread.
    *   **Explanation:** When a patient presents with asymmetric, linear, or clustered lesions on exposed skin areas, the diagnosis is almost certainly external (arthropod assault) rather than internal.

![[page492_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the tense, fluid-filled blister (bulla) atop an erythematous base.** This is an exaggerated localized hypersensitivity reaction known as *culicosis bullosa*.
    *   **Explanation:** This is most common in children or individuals with no prior exposure (immunonaive) to the insect (often fleas or mosquitoes). It represents an intense delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction, not an infection.
* **Assess for secondary infection.** While the fluid is usually sterile lymph, a ruptured bulla can be a portal of entry for bacteria.
    *   **Explanation:** Differentiate clear serous fluid (inflammatory) from cloudy purulent fluid (impetiginized/infected).

![[page494_img01.jpeg]]
* **Note the solitary, firm, erythematous nodule.** This represents a persistent insect bite reaction that has lasted weeks or months.
    *   **Explanation:** Physiologically, this is a pseudolymphoma. The immune system has failed to clear the antigen (or insect part) and has formed a lymphoid infiltrate to "wall it off," mimicking lymphoma histologically.
* **Check for excoriation (scratch marks) on the surface.** Chronic scratching can lead to lichenification (thickening of the skin).
    *   **Explanation:** The "itch-scratch cycle" perpetuates the lesion. Treatment often requires intralesional steroids to break the inflammatory cycle.

![[page496_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the "Matchbox Sign."** The image shows a container where a patient has collected lint, scabs, or debris to prove an infestation.
    *   **Explanation:** This is pathognomonic for Delusional Infestation (formerly Delusions of Parasitosis or Ekbom syndrome). The patient holds a fixed, false belief of being infested.
* **examine the contents microscopically.** You will find skin debris, clothing fibers, or harmless environmental insects, but no parasites.
    *   **Explanation:** This is a psychiatric condition, not a dermatologic one. Management involves building a therapeutic alliance and potentially using antipsychotic medication, rather than prescribing parasiticides.

![[page499_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the vector attached to the skin.** The arachnid (tick) is embedded, surrounded by a zone of erythema.
    *   **Explanation:** Ticks require a blood meal to molt or reproduce. They cement their mouthparts into the skin. Improper removal (squeezing the body) can inject more pathogens or leave mouthparts behind, causing a foreign body granuloma.
* **Monitor for vector-borne diseases.** The bite itself is often painless due to anesthetic saliva.
    *   **Explanation:** Depending on the region, ticks transmit Lyme disease (*Borrelia*), Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (*Rickettsia*), or Ehrlichiosis.

![[page500_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the targetoid (bullseye) lesion.** Note the expanding red ring with central clearing.
    *   **Explanation:** This is Erythema Migrans (EM), the pathognomonic early sign of Lyme disease. It expands centrifugally over days to weeks.
* **Understand the pathophysiology of the ring.** The bacteria (*Borrelia burgdorferi*) migrate outward from the bite site through the dermis.
    *   **Explanation:** The leading edge represents the active bacterial front and inflammation, while the center clears as the local immune response temporarily suppresses the bacteria there.

![[page501_img01.jpeg]]
* **Observe the central dusky necrosis.** The lesion shows a central area of dying tissue/ulceration surrounded by inflammation.
    *   **Explanation:** This is characteristic of bites from spiders with cytotoxic venom (e.g., *Loxosceles* species/Recluse spiders). The venom contains sphingomyelinase D, which destroys cell membranes and causes dermonecrosis.
* **Monitor for progression.** These wounds can be slow to heal and may require debridement if the eschar becomes extensive.
    *   **Explanation:** Pain is often delayed for hours after the bite. The reaction can range from mild irritation to deep tissue loss.

![[page503_img01.jpeg]]
* **Look for the linear burrow (cuniculus).** This is the wavy, thread-like, greyish line indicating the tunnel created by the mite.
    *   **Explanation:** The female scabies mite (*Sarcoptes scabiei*) burrows into the stratum corneum to lay eggs. This is the definitive clinical sign of scabies.
* **Check the classic anatomical distributions.** Note the location in the web spaces of the fingers or on the wrist.
    *   **Explanation:** Scabies mites prefer areas with thin stratum corneum and few hair follicles. Other common sites include the flexor wrists, axillae, umbilicus, and genitalia.

![[page505_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the reddish-brown, firm nodules.** These are "scabies nodules," often found in the axilla (armpit) or groin.
    *   **Explanation:** These are a hypersensitivity reaction to the mite antigens. Crucially, they can persist for months *after* the mites have been successfully killed.
* **Distinguish this from active infection.** The presence of nodules alone does not necessarily mean the treatment failed.
    *   **Explanation:** If there are no new burrows and the itching has decreased, these are likely post-scabetic nodules. They often require topical corticosteroids to resolve.

![[page507_img01.jpeg]]
* **Note the pustules and burrows on the sole of the foot.** In infants and young children, scabies can mimic pustular psoriasis or acropustulosis.
    *   **Explanation:** Unlike adults, infants have soft skin over their entire body. Therefore, scabies in infants often involves the palms, soles, face, and scalp—areas usually spared in adults.
* **Check for secondary bacterial infection.** The pustular appearance suggests a high risk of superinfection (Staph/Strep).
    *   **Explanation:** Because infants cannot scratch effectively, they may rub the feet together, leading to further irritation and spread.

![[page508_img01.jpeg]]
* **Observe the thick, hyperkeratotic, scaling plaques.** This is Crusted Scabies (formerly Norwegian Scabies).
    *   **Explanation:** This occurs in immunocompromised or neurologically impaired patients who cannot mechanically remove mites by scratching. The host immune response is insufficient to limit the mite population.
* **Be aware of the extreme contagiousness.** These crusts contain millions of mites (compared to 10-15 in classic scabies).
    *   **Explanation:** Unlike classic scabies, pruritus (itching) may be absent or mild. This form requires aggressive oral (ivermectin) and topical treatment and strict isolation.

![[page510_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the nits (eggs) on the hair shaft.** They are oval, white/yellow capsules firmly cemented to the hair.
    *   **Explanation:** Differentiate nits from dandruff (seborrheic dermatitis) or hair casts. Nits cannot be flicked off; they must be pulled down the entire shaft.
* **Estimate the duration of infestation.** Measure the distance of the nits from the scalp.
    *   **Explanation:** Hair grows approximately 1 cm per month. Nits are laid close to the scalp for warmth. Nits found several centimeters down the shaft indicate an infestation present for months.

![[page511_img01.jpeg]]
* **Examine the "Lice Eczema" pattern.** Note the erythema and excoriations on the posterior neck and upper back.
    *   **Explanation:** A rash in this distribution is highly suggestive of head lice (*Pediculosis capitis*). The rash is an allergic reaction to louse saliva and feces falling onto the neck.
* **Inspect the occipital scalp.** Lift the hair at the nape of the neck to find the lice or nits.
    *   **Explanation:** This area is the "warmest" and most protected part of the scalp, making it the preferred breeding ground for head lice.

![[page512_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the organism on the eyelashes.** This is *Phthiriasis palpebrarum*, an infestation of the eyelashes by pubic lice.
    *   **Explanation:** The pubic louse (*Phthirus pubis* or "crab louse") has wide claws adapted for grasping coarse hair (pubic, axillary, beard, eyelashes), unlike the head louse which grasps fine scalp hair.
* **Consider the mode of transmission.** In adults, this is sexually transmitted; in children, consider abuse but also innocent transmission (sleeping in parents' bed).
    *   **Explanation:** Mechanical removal or occlusion (using petroleum jelly) is often required as standard insecticides cannot be safely used near the eyes.

![[page513_img01.jpeg]]
* **Trace the serpiginous (snake-like) track.** The lesion is raised, red, and chaotic, moving a few millimeters to centimeters per day.
    *   **Explanation:** This is Cutaneous Larva Migrans (creeping eruption). It is caused by the larvae of animal hookworms (usually from dogs or cats, *Ancylostoma braziliense*).
* **Understand the "dead-end host" concept.** The larvae penetrate human skin but lack the enzymes to breach the basement membrane to enter the bloodstream.
    *   **Explanation:** They migrate aimlessly within the epidermis, causing intense itching, until they eventually die. Treatment (albendazole/ivermectin) speeds resolution and stops the itching.
