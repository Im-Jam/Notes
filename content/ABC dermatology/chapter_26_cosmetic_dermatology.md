* **Understand the "Inverted Triangle" of aging.** In youth, volume is high in the midface; with age, bone resorption and fat pad atrophy cause the face to lose structural support.
    *   **Physiological Note:** Aging is not just skin deep. The maxilla recedes, and the distinct malar fat pads atrophy and slide downward due to gravity and lax ligaments, creating jowls and nasolabial folds.
* **Identify surface-level cutaneous changes.** Note the pigmentary irregularities (dyschromia) and texture changes alongside volume loss.
    *   **Simplification:** Think of the aging face as a deflating balloon (volume loss) with a crumbling foundation (bone loss) and a worn-out fabric cover (photoaged skin).

![[page759_img01.jpeg]]
* **Memorize the Glogau Scale stages (I–IV).** This classification system quantifies photodamage to help determine appropriate treatment interventions.
    *   **Simplification:**
        *   **Type I (No Wrinkles):** "Perfect" skin, usually age 20–30.
        *   **Type II (Wrinkles in Motion):** Lines only appear when smiling/frowning.
        *   **Type III (Wrinkles at Rest):** Static lines are visible even without expression.
        *   **Type IV (Only Wrinkles):** Skin is yellow/gray, severe wrinkles everywhere; makeup cracks.
* **Correlate Glogau stage with treatment depth.** Higher stages usually require more aggressive resurfacing (lasers, deep peels) rather than just superficial care.

![[page760_img01.jpeg]]
* **Match filler rheology (thickness) to injection depth.** **A & B (Dermis):** Use low-viscosity, thin fillers for fine lines. **C & D (Subcutaneous/Periosteal):** Use high-viscosity, thick fillers for structural lift.
    *   **Physiology:** Placing a thick filler superficially results in lumps or the "Tyndall effect" (blue discoloration). Placing a thin filler deep on the bone will not provide adequate lift.
* **Identify the target planes.** **A:** Superficial dermis (fine etching). **D:** Periosteal (structural foundation).
    *   **Tip:** Periosteal placement (D) is safest regarding vascular occlusion in many areas because major vessels often run in the subcutaneous fat or deep fascia layers above this point.

![[page762_img01.jpeg]]
* **Locate the Vermillion Border for definition.** The needle is inserted at the junction of the red lip and white skin to enhance the shape and prevent "lipstick bleeding."
    *   **Physiological Note:** This technique restores the "white roll" of the lip.
* **Beware of vascular anatomy during lip injections.** The superior and inferior labial arteries usually run deep to the orbicularis oris muscle, but anatomical variations exist.
    *   **Safety Tip:** Always aspirate before injecting to ensure you are not in a vessel, which could cause necrosis. "Slow and steady" prevents vascular compression.

![[page764_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the Frontalis muscle as the target.** The frontalis is the large, vertically oriented muscle responsible for raising eyebrows and creating horizontal forehead lines.
    *   **Physiology:** Neuromodulators (like Botox) block acetylcholine release, relaxing the muscle.
* **Understand the risk of brow ptosis (drooping).** Since the frontalis is the *only* muscle that lifts the eyebrows, over-treating it or injecting too low (near the orbital rim) will cause the heavy brows to drop.
    *   **Simplification:** Keep injections to the upper half/two-thirds of the forehead to preserve some brow movement and prevent a heavy, angry look.

![[page765_img01.jpeg]]
* **Visualize the "Nefertiti Lift" technique.** Injections into the Platysma muscle (neck) reduce downward pull, sharpening the jawline.
    *   **Physiology:** The platysma is a depressor muscle; relaxing it allows the elevator muscles of the face to work unopposed, creating a subtle lift.
* **Recognize the Mentalis muscle injection.** This treats a dimpled, "cobblestone," or "peau d'orange" (orange peel) chin texture.
    *   **Extra Tip:** This also helps if the mentalis is hyperactive and curling the lower lip outward or pulling the chin up excessively.

![[page766_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the post-peel "bronzing" effect.** Solar lentigines (sun spots) often darken significantly and form a crust before peeling off after a TCA peel.
    *   **Student Note:** Patients must be warned that they will look "worse before better." This crusting is dead epidermal and dermal protein that will desquamate (shed) over 7–10 days.
* **Assess the efficacy of Medium Depth peels.** A 35% TCA peel penetrates to the papillary dermis, making it effective for pigment and moderate photoaging.

![[page767_img01.jpeg]]
* **Classify peel agents by penetration depth.**
    *   **Superficial:** AHAs (Glycolic), BHAs (Salicylic) – affects Stratum Corneum/Epidermis.
    *   **Medium:** TCA (35%) – affects Papillary Dermis.
    *   **Deep:** Phenol – affects Reticular Dermis.
* **Understand the clinical downtime correlation.** Deeper peels (Phenol) provide dramatic wrinkle reduction but carry high risks (scarring, hypopigmentation, cardiac arrhythmia) and long recovery times.
    *   **Physiology:** Deep peels essentially perform a "chemical face lift" by denaturing protein deep in the skin, forcing total regeneration of the collagen matrix.

![[page769_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify "Frosting" as the clinical endpoint for TCA.** The white appearance indicates protein coagulation (denaturation) and keratin precipitation.
    *   **Important Distinction:** Unlike glycolic acid, which must be neutralized with water/bicarbonate, TCA is self-neutralizing. The reaction stops when the frosting endpoint is reached.
* **Grade the frost to determine depth.**
    *   **Level 1:** Patchy white/erythema (Superficial).
    *   **Level 2:** Uniform white (Papillary Dermis).
    *   **Level 3:** Solid white enamel (Reticular Dermis – risk of scarring).

![[page771_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the danger of high-concentration TCA (>35-40%).** Spillage or using concentrations like 50% on non-scarred skin can cause immediate, full-thickness chemical burns and scarring.
    *   **Safety Protocol:** TCA concentrations above 35% are unpredictable and rarely used for full-face peeling anymore (replaced by Hetter/Phenol formulas).
* **Understand immediate management.** While TCA is self-neutralizing, immediate dilution with copious water is required for accidental spillage to minimize heat and depth of injury, followed by burn care.
