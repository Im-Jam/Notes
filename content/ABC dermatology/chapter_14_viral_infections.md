Here is the comprehensive study guide oriented around the image collection for Viral Infections.

![[page407_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the signs of primary inoculation.** Look for a localized, often indurated, inflammatory plaque or cluster of vesicles at a site of trauma or skin barrier breach.
    *   **Explanation:** This represents "Inoculation Herpes" (or Herpetic Whitlow if on the finger). Unlike recurrent herpes, which activates from a nerve root, this occurs when the virus is directly introduced into the skin through a cut or abrasion (common in contact sports like wrestling or in healthcare workers). The physiological response is a primary immune reaction at the entry site.

![[page408_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize the classic morphology of Herpes Labialis.** Note the grouped vesicles on an erythematous (red) base located at the vermilion border of the lip.
    *   **Explanation:** This is a reactivation of Herpes Simplex Virus 1 (HSV-1). The virus lies latent in the trigeminal ganglion and travels down the nerve axon upon reactivation (triggered by UV light, stress, or illness). The grouping of vesicles is the hallmark visual cue for herpes.

![[page409_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Check for herpes in non-mucosal areas.** Observe the cluster of crusted or fluid-filled vesicles on the ear (posterior pinna).
    *   **Explanation:** HSV can occur on keratinized skin, not just mucous membranes. While this looks similar to Herpes Zoster (Shingles), Zoster would typically involve the entire dermatome and be more painful. If this is recurrent in the exact same spot, it is HSV.

![[page410_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the "punched-out" erosions of Eczema Herpeticum.** Look for widespread, monomorphic (all look the same) punched-out ulcers or vesicles, particularly in areas of pre-existing atopic dermatitis.
    *   **Explanation:** This is a dermatologic emergency. Patients with eczema have a compromised skin barrier, allowing the HSV virus to spread rapidly across the skin surface (dissemination). It requires systemic antiviral treatment immediately to prevent bacterial superinfection or viremia.

![[page412_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Distinguish the "dewdrop on a rose petal" appearance.** Look for clear fluid-filled vesicles sitting on top of a red, inflamed area.
    *   **Explanation:** This is the primary infection of Varicella Zoster Virus (Chickenpox).
*   **Note the polymorphic nature of the rash.** Observe that lesions are in different stages of healing (some vesicles, some crusts, some papules) simultaneously.
    *   **Explanation:** This "starry sky" pattern distinguishes Chickenpox from Smallpox (where all lesions are at the same stage). In adults, primary Varicella is more severe and carries a higher risk of Varicella pneumonia.

![[page413_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the dermatomal distribution.** Note how the rash is strictly unilateral (one side of the body) and stops abruptly at the anatomical midline.
    *   **Explanation:** This is Herpes Zoster (Shingles), a reactivation of the Varicella virus from the dorsal root ganglion. The virus travels down a specific sensory nerve, so the rash traces the path of that skin nerve (dermatome). Pain or burning (prodrome) often precedes the visual rash by days.

![[page415_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize signs of immune compromise.** Observe the presence of zoster lesions spanning across more than two adjacent dermatomes or crossing the midline.
    *   **Explanation:** Multidermatomal or disseminated Zoster indicates that the host's cellular immunity is unable to contain the virus to a single nerve segment. This is a red flag for underlying immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, cancer, or immunosuppressive therapy).

![[page416_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Map the rash to cranial nerve anatomy.** Identify the rash distribution along the jawline and chin, corresponding to the V3 branch of the Trigeminal nerve.
    *   **Explanation:** Zoster can affect cranial nerves as well as spinal nerves. Involvement of the mandibular nerve (V3) may mimic dental pain initially. It is crucial to check the eye (V1 involvement) or ear (Ramsey Hunt syndrome) in facial Zoster cases.

![[page418_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Spot the "Herald Patch."** Look for a single, large, oval-shaped plaque with a central clearing and a collarette of scale (scaling just inside the edge).
    *   **Explanation:** This is the initial lesion of Pityriasis Rosea. It usually appears days or weeks before the generalized rash.
*   **Identify the "Christmas Tree" distribution.** Note how the smaller, secondary lesions on the back align with the skin tension lines (Langer's lines).
    *   **Explanation:** The rash slopes downwards from the spine, resembling the branches of a pine tree. This condition is self-limiting and possibly viral (HHV-6/7 link) but generally requires no treatment.

![[page420_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Look for central umbilication.** Identify the pearly, flesh-colored papules that have a small dimple or pit in the center.
    *   **Explanation:** This is the hallmark of Molluscum Contagiosum (a Poxvirus). It is highly contagious through skin-to-skin contact. The central core contains the viral particles.

![[page421_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify Henderson-Paterson bodies.** On the histology slide, look for large, eosinophilic (pink) cytoplasmic inclusion bodies within the keratinocytes.
    *   **Explanation:** These inclusion bodies are essentially "viral factories" pushing the nucleus of the cell to the side. They correspond to the cheesy white material that can be expressed from the center of a molluscum lesion.

![[page422_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize the inflammatory nodule of Orf.** Look for a solitary, target-like nodule (red center, white ring, red halo) often on the finger or hand.
    *   **Explanation:** This is a zoonotic Parapoxvirus infection transmitted from sheep or goats (look for patient history involving farming or petting zoos). It is self-limiting and should generally not be surgically excised.

![[page423_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify filiform architecture.** Note the finger-like, thread-like projections growing out from the skin surface.
    *   **Explanation:** These are Filiform Warts (Verruca Filiformis), a variant of HPV warts common on the face and neck. The shape is due to the virus causing rapid growth of the epidermis in narrow columns.

![[page424_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Assess for periungual hyperkeratosis.** Look for rough, thickened skin growing around or under the nail plate.
    *   **Explanation:** Periungual warts can be difficult to treat because they may extend under the nail matrix. They can cause nail dystrophy (deformation) by disrupting the nail growth center.

![[page425_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Differentiate plantar warts from corns.** Look for the disruption of normal skin ridges (fingerprints/footprints) and the presence of thrombosed capillaries.
    *   **Explanation:** If you pare down (shave) the surface, you will see "black dots"—these are tiny blood vessels that have clotted, which distinguishes a viral wart from a corn (callus). Plantar warts grow inward due to the pressure of walking.

![[page429_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Spot the pathognomonic Koplik's spots.** Look for small white or blue-white specks on a red background inside the mouth (buccal mucosa), resembling "grains of salt."
    *   **Explanation:** These appear *before* the skin rash of Measles (Rubeola). Finding these allows for early diagnosis during the highly contagious prodromal phase (fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis).

![[page430_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the morbilliform eruption.** Observe the erythematous, maculopapular rash that creates a confluent (blending together) redness.
    *   **Explanation:** The Measles rash characteristically starts at the hairline/ears and spreads downward (cephalocaudal spread) over the body. It typically fades in the same order it appeared.

![[page431_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Contrast Rubella with Measles.** Note the fainter, discrete pink macules that are less likely to merge (confluent) compared to Measles.
    *   **Explanation:** Rubella (German Measles) is often milder clinically but is dangerous due to teratogenicity (congenital defects) in pregnancy. Look for tender lymph nodes at the back of the neck (occipital lymphadenopathy) as a diagnostic clue.

![[page433_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize the acral distribution.** Look for a papular rash located symmetrically on the face, buttocks, and limbs, while the trunk remains relatively clear.
    *   **Explanation:** This is Gianotti–Crosti syndrome (Papular Acrodermatitis of Childhood). It is a hypersensitivity reaction to a viral infection (commonly EBV or Hepatitis B), not the direct infection of the skin itself.

![[page435_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identify the "football" vesicle shape.** Look for oval, grey-white vesicles with a red halo on the palms or soles.
    *   **Explanation:** This is the classic presentation of Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD) caused by Coxsackie A16. The distinct oval shape runs parallel to the skin lines.

![[page436_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Recognize "Atypical" HFMD.** Observe the more extensive, crusted, and widespread rash that may look like eczema (Eczema Coxsackium).
    *   **Explanation:** This severe form is caused by Coxsackie A6. Unlike the mild A16 strain, A6 causes high fever and widespread lesions that can involve the face, trunk, and extremities, often mimicking chickenpox or eczema herpeticum.

![[page437_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Link nail shedding to prior infection.** Identify proximal separation of the nail plate from the nail bed (Onychomadesis).
    *   **Explanation:** This occurs 1–2 months *after* a recognizable infection, most commonly Coxsackie A6 (HFMD). The viral illness causes a temporary arrest in nail matrix activity; as the nail grows out, the gap becomes visible and the nail sheds. It is temporary and the nail will regrow.
