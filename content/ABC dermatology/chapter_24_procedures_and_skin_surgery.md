* **Standard Cryotherapy Application.** This image demonstrates the application of liquid nitrogen using a spray gun device, held perpendicular to the lesion.
* **Mechanism of Action.** The physiological goal is targeted tissue destruction through rapid freezing and slow thawing, which causes intracellular ice crystal formation and cell membrane rupture.
* **Clinical Tip:** Aim for a "halo" of frost 1-2mm beyond the visible lesion margins for benign lesions like warts, but wider for premalignant lesions. A "double freeze-thaw" cycle is often more effective than a single long freeze.

![[page720_img01.jpeg]]
* **Electrodessication and Hemostasis.** The figure shows the use of a hyfrecator or electrosurgical unit to apply high-voltage, low-amperage current to tissue.
* **Physiological Effect.** This process dehydrates and chars superficial tissue (dessication) and seals blood vessels (hemostasis/coagulation) via heat generation.
* **Clinical Tip:** This is commonly used after curettage (C&C). Ensure the patient does not have an implantable cardiac device (pacemaker) before starting, or use bipolar forceps to restrict the current flow.

![[page721_img01.jpeg]]
* **Curettage Instruments.** Shown are the spoon curette (a) and ring curette (b), sharp-edged tools used for scraping soft or friable skin lesions.
* **Tactile Feedback.** The success of this procedure relies on the physiological difference in texture; tumor tissue is often softer and "mushy," while the underlying healthy dermis feels firm and "gritty."
* **Clinical Tip:** The spoon curette is ideal for scooping out bulkier lesions, while the ring curette allows for multi-directional scraping. This technique is often used for Seborrheic Keratoses or superficial Basal Cell Carcinomas.

![[page724_img01.jpeg]]
* **Punch Biopsy Instrumentation.** This image displays standard disposable punch biopsy tools, which feature a circular hollow blade ranging from 2mm to 8mm in diameter.
* **Selection Criteria.** Choose a diameter that encompasses the most representative part of the rash or lesion; 3mm or 4mm is the standard for most diagnostic biopsies.
* **Simplification:** Think of this as a "cookie cutter" for the skin that allows the pathologist to view the full thickness of the epidermis, dermis, and upper subcutis.

![[page725_img01.jpeg]]
* **Local Anesthesia Administration.** The needle is inserted at a shallow angle to raise an intradermal bleb using Lidocaine (often with Epinephrine).
* **Physiological Effect.** Epinephrine causes vasoconstriction, which significantly reduces bleeding during the procedure and prolongs the anesthetic effect.
* **Clinical Tip:** Injecting slowly and using buffered lidocaine (mixed with bicarbonate) reduces the "stinging" burn sensation for the patient.

![[page726_img01.jpeg]]
* **Punch Tool Insertion Technique.** The instrument is held perpendicular to the skin tension lines and rotated in a single direction with firm downward pressure.
* **Depth Control.** The blade must penetrate through the dermis into the subcutaneous fat to ensure the sample releases easily.
* **Clinical Tip:** Twisting back and forth (oscillating) can shred the tissue edges; continuous rotation in one direction produces a cleaner cut. Stretching the skin perpendicular to skin lines before punching creates an oval defect that sutures closed more easily.

![[page727_img01.jpeg]]
* **Visualization of the Tissue Plug.** Once the punch tool is removed, a cylindrical plug of skin remains, distinct from the surrounding tissue.
* **Anatomy Check.** You should see the yellowish subcutaneous fat at the base of the plug, indicating sufficient depth was achieved.
* **Simplification:** If the plug does not "pop up," the punch likely did not go deep enough into the fat layer, and you may need to re-insert the tool carefully.

![[page728_img01.jpeg]]
* **Elevating the Specimen.** Forceps are used to gently lift the skin plug upwards to expose the connective tissue base.
* **Handling Precaution.** Do not use toothed forceps to squeeze the center of the specimen, as this causes "crush artifact" which destroys cellular details needed for diagnosis.
* **Clinical Tip:** Grasp the specimen only by the very edge of the epidermis or use a needle to spear the edge to lift it without crushing.

![[page729_img01.jpeg]]
* **Severing the Base.** Curved iris scissors are used to cut through the subcutaneous fat at the base of the plug to free the specimen.
* **Sample Preservation.** Immediately place the specimen into a fixative jar (usually formalin) to prevent autolysis (tissue degradation).
* **Simplification:** This leaves a small circular defect which can either be sutured with a single stitch for faster healing or left to heal by secondary intention (granulation).

![[page730_img01.jpeg]]
* **Incisional Biopsy Marking.** An elliptical or wedge shape is marked across a portion of a large lesion, rather than removing the whole thing.
* **Diagnostic Utility.** This is used when a lesion is too large for a punch or simple excision, or when the diagnosis (e.g., vasculitis or panniculitis) requires a deep wedge of fat.
* **Clinical Tip:** The incision should include both the pathology (the lesion) and a small rim of normal skin to allow the pathologist to compare the transition zone.

![[page731_img01.jpeg]]
* **Skin Tension Lines (Trunk).** This diagram maps the Relaxed Skin Tension Lines (RSTL) or Langer’s lines on the chest and back.
* **Surgical Planning.** Incisions made parallel to these lines heal with minimal tension and scarring; incisions perpendicular to them tend to gape and form hypertrophic scars.
* **Simplification:** On the trunk, lines generally run horizontally. Always pinch the skin to confirm the direction of loose folds before cutting.

![[page732_img01.jpeg]]
* **Skin Tension Lines (Limbs).** This diagram illustrates the tension lines on the arms and legs, which generally run circumferentially or spirally.
* **Physiological Stress.** Muscles flex and extend the limbs, creating significant tension; vertical incisions on the lower leg heal particularly poorly.
* **Clinical Tip:** When excising a lesion on a limb, orient the long axis of your ellipse along these lines (essentially wrapping around the limb) rather than up and down the limb.

![[page733_img01.jpeg]]
* **Skin Tension Lines (Face).** Facial lines follow the muscles of expression, circling the eyes and mouth and running horizontally on the forehead.
* **Cosmetic Importance.** Following these lines is critical on the face to hide surgical scars within natural wrinkle lines.
* **Simplification:** Ask the patient to smile, frown, or raise their eyebrows to visualize these lines dynamically before marking your surgical site.

![[page734_img01.jpeg]]
* **Excision Design for BCC.** An ellipse (fusiform) shape is drawn around the lesion with a 3:1 length-to-width ratio.
* **Safety Margins.** For a Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC), a 4mm margin of clinically normal skin is included to ensure complete removal of microscopic tumor extensions.
* **Physiological Point:** The 3:1 ratio is required to prevent "dog ears" (standing cones of excess skin) at the ends of the wound when it is sutured closed.

![[page735_img01.jpeg]]
* **Defect Visualization.** The specimen has been removed down to the subcutaneous fat, leaving a boat-shaped defect.
* **Undermining.** The surgeon often cuts horizontally under the skin edges (undermining) to separate the dermis from the underlying fascia.
* **Clinical Tip:** Undermining releases tension, allowing the edges to be brought together easily without stretching the skin, which improves blood flow and healing.

![[page736_img01.jpeg]]
* **Wound Closure and Eversion.** The wound is sutured, resulting in a ridge of skin that pouts outward (eversion).
* **Physiology of Healing.** As scars mature, they contract and flatten. A wound that starts flat will eventually invert (sink), creating a shadow and a visible scar.
* **Simplification:** "Eversion is Excellence." The wound should look like a small ridge or a tent immediately after surgery to ensure a flat, invisible scar months later.

![[page738_img01.jpeg]]
* **Epidermal Suture Placement.** This illustrates the needle entry angle (90 degrees) for simple interrupted sutures.
* **Technique Precision.** The needle should enter perpendicular to the skin surface to capture a rectangular bite of tissue, rather than a triangular one, promoting eversion.
* **Clinical Tip:** These sutures align the top layer of skin (epidermis) but provide little long-term tensile strength. They are usually removed in 5-14 days depending on the body site.

![[page740_img01.jpeg]]
* **Buried Dermal Sutures.** This shows the placement of absorbable sutures deep within the dermis using an inverted knot technique.
* **Tension Reduction.** These sutures take all the tension off the wound edges, holding the deep layers together so the epidermal sutures can just gently align the surface.
* **Clinical Tip:** The knot is tied at the bottom of the loop (deepest point) so the bulk of the knot is buried away from the skin surface, preventing it from spitting out or causing irritation. This is often called the "heart stitch" or "flask stitch."
