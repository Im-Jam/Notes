Here is a comprehensive study guide based on the image collection, oriented towards clinical recognition, pathophysiology, and practical management of fungal infections.

![[page465_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the heightened inflammatory response typical of zoophilic dermatophytes.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is "Animal Ringworm." Unlike human-adapted (anthropophilic) fungi which often cause mild scaling, animal-adapted (zoophilic) fungi cause intense inflammation because the human immune system recognizes them as foreign invaders and attacks vigorously.
* **Look for a history of contact with kittens, puppies, or farm animals.**
    *   **Tip:** If you see a boggy, pustular, or highly inflamed fungal lesion, ask about new pets. Treatment often requires oral antifungals due to the depth of inflammation.

![[page466_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the classic "grey patch" tinea capitis pattern.**
    *   **Explanation:** *Microsporum* species often cause an **ectothrix** infection (fungal spores coat the *outside* of the hair shaft). This leads to hair breaking off slightly above the scalp, leaving a scaly, grey-appearing patch of alopecia.
* **Remember the Wood’s Lamp diagnostic tip.**
    *   **Tip:** *Microsporum* species (like *M. canis* or *M. audouinii*) typically fluoresce **bright green** under a Wood's lamp (UV light). *Trichophyton* species usually do not.

![[page467_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the "black dot" alopecia characteristic of *Trichophyton tonsurans*.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is an **endothrix** infection (fungus invades *inside* the hair shaft), making the hair extremely brittle. The hairs break flush with the scalp surface, creating the appearance of black dots within the patches of hair loss.
* **Note the subtle nature of the infection.**
    *   **Simplification:** Unlike the "grey patch" of *Microsporum*, this presents with less obvious scaling and can be mistaken for alopecia areata or trichotillomania. It does **not** fluoresce under Wood's lamp.

![[page468_img01.jpeg]]
* **Observe the "moth-eaten" alopecia spreading beyond the scalp.**
    *   **Explanation:** Tinea capitis is not strictly limited to the head; the organism (*T. tonsurans*) can shed and infect adjacent skin (neck/shoulders).
* **Check for Posterior Cervical Lymphadenopathy.**
    *   **Tip:** Significant lymph node swelling in the back of the neck is a hallmark sign of tinea capitis. If a child has a flaky scalp and swollen neck nodes, treat for fungus until proven otherwise.

![[page469_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize a Kerion: a painful, boggy, purulent mass.**
    *   **Explanation:** This looks like a bacterial abscess, but it is actually a severe T-cell mediated hypersensitivity reaction to the fungus. The "pus" is often sterile inflammation.
* **Do NOT incision and drain.**
    *   **Tip:** Cutting into a kerion is a common mistake; it provides no relief and increases scarring. Treat with oral antifungals and consider a short course of oral steroids to reduce inflammation and prevent permanent hair loss (scarring alopecia).

![[page470_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify an 'Id reaction' (Dermatophytid) on the hands or body.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is an autoimmune-like eczema response to fungal antigens circulating in the blood. It occurs at a site distant from the actual infection, often shortly after starting antifungal treatment.
* **Understand that the rash itself is sterile.**
    *   **Simplification:** The fungus is on the head (or feet), but the rash is on the fingers. Do not stop the antifungal medication. Treat the 'Id' rash with topical steroids and moisturizers while the antifungal clears the source.

![[page471_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Tinea Incognito: Ringworm masked by topical steroids.**
    *   **Explanation:** When a fungal infection is mistakenly treated with steroids (hydrocortisone/betamethasone), the local immune response is suppressed. The redness and scaling decrease, but the fungus thrives and spreads unchecked.
* **Look for loss of the "active border."**
    *   **Tip:** The classic ring shape disappears, and the rash becomes diffuse, often with prominent pustules or folliculitis. If a rash looks "weird" and gets worse with steroids, scrape it for fungus.

![[page472_img01.jpeg]]
* **Differentiate Seborrhoeic Dermatitis from Tinea.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is an inflammatory reaction to *Malassezia* yeast (a normal skin inhabitant), not a contagious infection. It favors sebum-rich areas: scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, and chest.
* **Look for "greasy" yellow scales.**
    *   **Simplification:** Tinea usually has a clear center and a raised border. Seborrhoeic dermatitis is diffuse, symmetric, and greasy.

![[page473_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the "Moccasin" distribution of chronic Tinea Pedis.**
    *   **Explanation:** Caused often by *T. rubrum*, this presents as diffuse, dry, powdery scaling on the soles and sides of the feet (like a slipper).
* **Check for the "One Hand, Two Feet" syndrome.**
    *   **Tip:** Patients often pick at their feet, transferring the fungus to *one* hand. If you see scaling on both feet, check the hands.

![[page473_img02.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Interdigital Tinea Pedis (Toeweb infection).**
    *   **Explanation:** Maceration (wet, white skin) between the toes, usually the 4th and 5th web space. The skin barrier breakdown here is a major entry point for bacteria, leading to lower leg cellulitis.
* **Management requires drying agents.**
    *   **Simplification:** Antifungal creams fail if the area remains wet. Use powders, separate the toes, and treat the underlying fungus to prevent bacterial superinfection.

![[page475_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the classic annular lesion of Tinea Corporis.**
    *   **Explanation:** The "Ringworm." The fungus lives in the keratin (dead skin layer). As it eats outward, the center heals/clears, and the active inflammation remains at the periphery.
* **Scrape the edge, not the center.**
    *   **Tip:** When doing a KOH prep or culture, take scales from the raised, red, leading edge. The center often contains no viable fungus.

![[page476_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Tinea Cruris ("Jock Itch") by its sharp demarcation.**
    *   **Explanation:** An intensely itchy, red rash in the groin folds spreading down the inner thigh.
* **Apply the "Scrotal Sparing" rule.**
    *   **Simplification:** Dermatophytes (Tinea) generally **spare** the scrotum/penis skin. If the scrotum is red and inflamed, it is likely **Candida** (yeast) or psoriasis, not Tinea.

![[page478_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify hyperpigmented Pityriasis Versicolor.**
    *   **Explanation:** Caused by the overgrowth of the commensal yeast *Malassezia furfur*. It presents as brown or salmon-colored scaly macules, often on the trunk of young adults.
* **Perform the "Scratch Test."**
    *   **Tip:** The scaling is very fine. If you scratch a lesion with a fingernail, you will see a subtle flake (Evocative Scale Sign). Microscopy shows "spaghetti and meatballs" (hyphae and spores).

![[page479_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify hypopigmented Pityriasis Versicolor.**
    *   **Explanation:** The yeast produces azelaic acid, which inhibits melanin production by melanocytes. This leaves white spots that do not tan.
* **Manage expectations regarding repigmentation.**
    *   **Tip:** Even after the fungus is killed, the white spots will remain until the skin naturally regenerates its pigment (weeks to months). It does not mean the treatment failed.

![[page480_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Distal Lateral Subungual Onychomycosis.**
    *   **Explanation:** *T. rubrum* invades the nail bed from the distal end. The nail plate becomes thickened, yellow, and separated from the nail bed (onycholysis) by subungual debris.
* **Treat systemically.**
    *   **Simplification:** Topical lacquers rarely penetrate thick nails effectively. Oral terbinafine is the gold standard but requires liver function monitoring.

![[page481_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish Candida Onychomycosis from Dermatophyte infection.**
    *   **Explanation:** Candida attacks the **soft tissue** first (chronic paronychia). Look for a swollen, red, tender proximal nail fold and loss of the cuticle *before* the nail becomes dystrophic.
* **Assess for "Wet Work."**
    *   **Tip:** This is common in bartenders, cleaners, or healthcare workers whose hands are constantly in water. Keeping hands dry is as important as the antifungal.

![[page483_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Cutaneous Candidiasis (Intertrigo).**
    *   **Explanation:** Candida loves warm, moist environments (groin, under breasts). Unlike Tinea, the rash is beefy red, moist, and involves the depths of the skin creases.
* **Look for Satellite Lesions.**
    *   **Tip:** Small red papules or pustules scattered just outside the main red plaque are pathognomonic for Candida infection.

![[page484_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize Oral Candidiasis (Thrush).**
    *   **Explanation:** White, curd-like plaques on the mucosa that can be wiped off, leaving a bleeding red base.
* **Investigate the underlying cause.**
    *   **Simplification:** Thrush is rare in healthy adults. Look for: inhaled steroid use (asthma), recent antibiotics, diabetes, or immunosuppression (HIV).

![[page485_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Disseminated Fusarium infection.**
    *   **Explanation:** *Fusarium* is a mold found in soil/plants. In severely immunocompromised patients (like bone marrow recipients), it enters the blood.
* **Recognize these as "embolic" skin lesions.**
    *   **Physiology:** The skin lesions (often painful, necrotic nodules with a halo) are caused by fungi traveling through the bloodstream and blocking small blood vessels in the skin. This indicates a medical emergency with high mortality.

![[page486_img01.jpeg]]
* **Recognize the appearance of Deep/Subcutaneous Fungal Infections.**
    *   **Explanation:** Organisms like Sporothrix or Madurella are introduced via trauma (e.g., thorn prick). They cause nodules, ulcers, or sinus tracts that may drain granules.
* **Suspect this in non-healing wounds.**
    *   **Tip:** If an ulcer or nodule fails to heal with antibiotics, especially on the extremities of a gardener or farmer, perform a tissue biopsy for fungal culture.
