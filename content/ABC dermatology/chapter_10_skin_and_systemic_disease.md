Here is a comprehensive study guide based on the images provided in Chapter 10, designed to orient students to key dermatological signs of systemic disease.

![[page280_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is a classic Toxic Erythema, specifically a morbilliform (measles-like) drug eruption.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is the most common type of cutaneous drug reaction. It is a Type IV (T-cell mediated) hypersensitivity reaction. "Morbilliform" describes the pattern of erythematous macules and papules that often start on the trunk and spread to the extremities.
* **The rash typically appears 7–14 days after starting a new medication.**
    *   **Simplification:** If you see a generalized red rash in a hospitalized patient, check the drug chart first (antibiotics and anticonvulsants are frequent culprits).

![[page281_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify this as Erythema Multiforme (EM) by the hallmark "target" or "iris" lesions.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Look for the three concentric zones: a central dusky/blistered area, a pale edematous ring, and a peripheral red halo.
* **The most common trigger is infection, specifically Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV).**
    *   **Simplification:** While drugs can cause this, HSV is the #1 cause of Minor EM. If mucosal membranes are severely involved, consider Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS), though they are now considered distinct entities.

![[page283_img01.jpeg]]
* **This presents as Erythema Nodosum: tender, ill-defined, erythematous nodules usually found on the anterior shins.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is a septal panniculitis (inflammation of the fat septa). The skin surface is smooth, not scaly/ulcerated.
* **Remember the mnemonic "NODOSUM" for causes: NO cause (idiopathic), Drugs (OCP, sulfonamides), Oral contraceptives, Sarcoidosis, Ulcerative colitis (IBD), Microbiology (Strep, TB).**
    *   **Simplification:** Painful red bumps on the shins? Think Strep throat, Sarcoidosis, or Inflammatory Bowel Disease.

![[page284_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Annular Erythema, characterized by ring-shaped lesions with central clearing.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** A specific type is *Erythema Annulare Centrifugum* (EAC), where the ring expands outwards. Look for a "trailing scale" (scale on the inner edge of the ring).
* **It is a reactive process often linked to underlying infections (fungal/tinea) or rarely malignancy.**
    *   **Simplification:** Always rule out Tinea (ringworm) first with a scraping; if negative, look for systemic triggers like Lyme disease (Erythema Migrans) or autoimmune issues.

![[page285_img01.jpeg]]
* **This shows Cutaneous Sarcoidosis, often presenting as reddish-brown papules or plaques on the face.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** These contain non-caseating granulomas. If you press a glass slide against them (diascopy), they appear "apple-jelly" colored.
* **A specific variant on the nose/cheeks is called "Lupus Pernio" and indicates a high risk of respiratory tract involvement.**
    *   **Simplification:** Sarcoidosis is the "great imitator." Always check a Chest X-Ray for hilar lymphadenopathy if you suspect this.

![[page286_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Melasma, presenting as symmetrical, irregular hyperpigmentation on sun-exposed areas of the face.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It is driven by Hormones + UV Light. Melanocytes produce excessive pigment.
* **It is strongly associated with pregnancy ("Mask of Pregnancy") and Oral Contraceptive Pills.**
    *   **Simplification:** Treatment is difficult if the patient does not strictly avoid the sun. The pigment sits in the dermis/epidermis and fades slowly after stopping hormones.

![[page287_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Acanthosis Nigricans by the velvety, hyperpigmented thickening of the skin, usually in axillary or neck folds.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is a cutaneous marker for tissue insulin resistance. High insulin levels stimulate keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation.
* **While usually benign (associated with Obesity/Type 2 Diabetes), a sudden, severe onset can signal gastric malignancy.**
    *   **Simplification:** Dirty-looking neck? Don't scrub it. Check the patient's HbA1c (blood sugar).

![[page289_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Necrobiosis Lipoidica, featuring yellow-brown, atrophic (waxy/shiny) plaques with telangiectasia on the shins.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** There is collagen degeneration (necrobiosis) and granulomatous inflammation. The center is depressed and can ulcerate.
* **There is a very strong association with Diabetes Mellitus.**
    *   **Simplification:** Differentiate from Erythema Nodosum: EN is lumpy/painful/red; Necrobiosis Lipoidica is flat/shiny/yellow/painless (unless ulcerated).

![[page291_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Granuloma Annulare, appearing as a ring of smooth, flesh-colored or red papules without scale.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Like Necrobiosis Lipoidica, this involves collagen degeneration, but it is usually self-limiting and common in children/young adults.
* **It is often mistaken for Ringworm (Tinea), but Tinea is scaly; Granuloma Annulare is smooth.**
    *   **Simplification:** "Ring without scale." It often disappears spontaneously within 2 years.

![[page293_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Pretibial Myxoedema, presenting as waxy, indurated, "peau d'orange" plaques on the shins.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It is caused by the deposition of hyaluronic acid (mucin) in the dermis.
* **It is a specific manifestation of Graves' Disease (Autoimmune Hyperthyroidism).**
    *   **Simplification:** Look for the triad: Goitre + Exophthalmos (bulging eyes) + Pretibial Myxoedema (thyroid dermopathy).

![[page295_img01.jpeg]]
* **This depicts Zinc Deficiency (Acrodermatitis Enteropathica), showing crusted, red dermatitis around the mouth (periorificial) and extremities.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Zinc is essential for metalloenzymes used in skin repair.
* **This can be genetic (defect in zinc absorption) or acquired (alcoholism, malabsorption syndromes).**
    *   **Simplification:** "Periorificial dermatitis" + Diarrhea + Hair loss = Check Zinc levels.

![[page297_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Shiitake Mushroom Dermatitis, characterized by linear "whip-like" (flagellate) erythematous streaks.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It is a toxic reaction to *lentinan*, a polysaccharide found in raw or undercooked shiitake mushrooms. It is heat-labile (cooking destroys the toxin).
* **The rash appears 24–48 hours after ingestion and is very itchy.**
    *   **Simplification:** It looks like the patient has been whipped or scratched, but the history of a restaurant meal reveals the cause.

![[page297_img02.jpeg]]
* **This is Pyoderma Gangrenosum, a rapidly enlarging, painful ulcer with a violaceous (purple), undermined border.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is a neutrophilic dermatosis, not an infection. It is associated with IBD (Ulcerative Colitis/Crohn's), Rheumatoid Arthritis, and myeloma.
* **Crucial Tip: Do NOT debride surgically. It exhibits "Pathergy," meaning trauma makes the ulcer grow larger.**
    *   **Simplification:** A "necrotic" looking wound that gets worse with surgery? Stop cutting and start immunosuppression (steroids).

![[page298_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Dermatitis Herpetiformis, showing intensely itchy, grouped vesicles/blisters on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees, buttocks).**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It is caused by IgA deposits in the dermal papillae.
* **It is the cutaneous manifestation of Coeliac Disease (Gluten sensitive enteropathy).**
    *   **Simplification:** The "Suicide Itch." Even if the patient has no gut symptoms, they have Celiac disease. Treatment is a Gluten-Free Diet + Dapsone.

![[page300_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Porphyria Cutanea Tarda (PCT), presenting with blisters, fragility, and scarring on sun-exposed dorsal hands.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** A defect in the heme synthesis pathway (uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase) leads to phototoxic porphyrins building up in the skin.
* **Common triggers include Alcohol, Hepatitis C, and Estrogens.**
    *   **Simplification:** Urine glows coral-pink under a Wood's lamp. It is often called the "Werewolf disease" due to hypertrichosis (hair growth) on the face.

![[page301_img01.jpeg]]
* **These are Xanthomas (in Alagille Syndrome), which are deposits of lipid-laden macrophages in the skin.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** They appear as firm, yellowish nodules. While Alagille is a genetic liver syndrome causing cholestasis, xanthomas generally indicate hyperlipidemia.
* **The location of xanthomas can hint at the type of lipid abnormality (e.g., tendon xanthomas = high cholesterol).**
    *   **Simplification:** Yellow bumps = Check lipid panel and liver function.

![[page303_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Piebaldism, a genetic condition characterized by a white forelock of hair and stable patches of depigmentation.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It is Autosomal Dominant (KIT mutation) causing defective melanocyte migration.
* **Unlike Vitiligo, these patches are present at birth and do not expand.**
    *   **Simplification:** The "White Forelock" is the giveaway. The depigmented patches often contain islands of normal pigment within them.

![[page304_img01.jpeg]]
* **This shows Vitiligo, an acquired autoimmune condition causing complete loss of pigment (melanocytes).**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** The image shows treatment with Narrowband UVB (TL-01), which immunosuppresses the skin and stimulates dormant melanocytes in hair follicles to repigment.
* **Lesions are chalk-white and often symmetrical.**
    *   **Simplification:** Look for the "Koebner Phenomenon"—vitiligo appearing in areas of trauma/scratching.

![[page306_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Haemochromatosis, known as "Bronze Diabetes," causing generalized hyperpigmentation.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Genetic iron overload leads to iron (hemosiderin) deposition in the skin and stimulation of melanin production.
* **The skin takes on a metallic grey or bronze hue.**
    *   **Simplification:** If a patient is tanned without sun exposure and has new-onset diabetes or liver issues, check Ferritin/Iron studies.

![[page308_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Sézary Syndrome, the leukemic phase of Cutaneous T-Cell Lymphoma (CTCL).**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** The patient presents with Erythroderma (whole body redness), lymphadenopathy, and atypical T-cells (Sézary cells) in the blood.
* **It is distinct from Mycosis Fungoides (which consists of patches/plaques) because the cancer cells are circulating in the blood.**
    *   **Simplification:** A "Red Man" with swollen lymph nodes and intense itching needs a blood film and skin biopsy.

![[page309_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is a Cutaneous B-Cell Lymphoma, presenting as smooth, plum-colored nodules or plaques.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** These are malignant proliferations of B-lymphocytes in the skin. The surface is usually intact (unlike ulcers).
* **Prognosis is generally better than systemic B-cell lymphomas or aggressive T-cell lymphomas.**
    *   **Simplification:** "Plum-colored nodule" is a classic description for lymphoma or sarcoidosis. Biopsy is mandatory.

![[page311_img01.jpeg]]
* **This shows Poikiloderma, a descriptive term for skin showing three features: Atrophy (thinning), Reticulate Hyperpigmentation, and Telangiectasia.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is a sign of damage, not a specific disease.
* **Causes include chronic sun damage (Poikiloderma of Civatte), Dermatomyositis, or Cutaneous T-Cell Lymphoma.**
    *   **Simplification:** If you see "mottled" skin with these three features, look for the underlying cause (Connective tissue disease vs. Sun damage).

![[page312_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Polymorphous Eruption of Pregnancy (PEP), formerly PUPPP.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** It presents as itchy urticarial papules within abdominal striae (stretch marks).
* **Key clinical distinction: It usually SPARES the umbilicus (belly button).**
    *   **Simplification:** Occurs in late pregnancy (3rd trimester), usually first pregnancy. It is annoying (itchy) but harmless to the baby.

![[page314_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Pemphigoid Gestationis, an autoimmune blistering disease of pregnancy.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Caused by antibodies against the basement membrane (BP180). Unlike PEP, this **involves** the umbilicus and can be bullous (blistering).
* **It carries a risk to the fetus (prematurity, small for gestational age).**
    *   **Simplification:** Blisters around the belly button in a pregnant woman = High Risk. Needs specialist care.

![[page317_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is Ichthyosis Vulgaris, a genetic condition causing dry, rectangular scales resembling fish skin.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** Caused by a mutation in the Filaggrin gene (crucial for skin barrier).
* **It is strongly associated with Atopic Eczema and keratosis pilaris.**
    *   **Simplification:** Usually spares the flexures (folds). Palms will show "hyperlinearity" (extra skin creases).

![[page319_img01.jpeg]]
* **These lines represent Blaschko's Lines, the embryonic migration pathways of skin cells.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** They are V-shaped on the back and S-shaped on the abdomen. They do *not* follow nerves (dermatomes) or blood vessels.
* **Skin lesions following these lines suggest Mosaicism (two genetically different cell populations in one body).**
    *   **Simplification:** If a rash follows a swirl pattern, it is likely a genetic/congenital anomaly.

![[page320_img01.jpeg]]
* **This is an Epidermal Naevus following Blaschko's lines.**
    *   **Physiology & Tips:** This is a hamartoma (overgrowth of normal tissue) of the epidermis.
* **Because it follows Blaschko's lines, it indicates a somatic mutation that occurred during fetal development.**
    *   **Simplification:** Warty, brown streaks arranged in swirls. Usually present at birth or early childhood.
