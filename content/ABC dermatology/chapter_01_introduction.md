Here is a comprehensive study guide based on the visual collection from Chapter 1. These notes cover primary and secondary skin lesions, morphological patterns, and key pathological examples.

![[page040_img01.jpeg]]
* **Observe the classic plaque morphology and associated nail dystrophy.** A plaque is a raised, flat-topped lesion >1 cm in diameter, here covered in silvery scale (psoriasis).
* **Check the nails for pitting, onycholysis (lifting), and oil spots.** In students' exams, nail changes are often the "clue" to diagnosing psoriasis when the skin rash is ambiguous.
* **Physiological context:** Psoriasis is a hyperproliferative disorder where the skin turnover time is drastically reduced (from 28 days to ~4 days), leading to the accumulation of immature skin cells (scale) and thickened epidermis (acanthosis).

![[page041_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the deep, ill-defined red nodules on the anterior shins.** This is the classic presentation of Erythema Nodosum, often tender to palpation.
* **Note the association with pregnancy, though it can also be triggered by sarcoidosis, streptococcal infections, or drugs.** 
* **Physiological context:** This represents *panniculitis*—inflammation of the subcutaneous fat. Because the inflammation is deep under the dermis, the borders are indistinct (you feel it better than you see it) and surface changes like scale are absent.

![[page043_img01.jpeg]]
* **Apply the ABCDE rule to this pigmented lesion: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter >6mm, and Evolution.** This is a superficial spreading melanoma.
* **Look for the "ugly duckling" sign.** If a patient has many moles, this one stands out because it looks different from the rest.
* **Physiological context:** This is a malignancy of melanocytes (pigment-producing cells). "Superficial spreading" implies a radial growth phase before it invades vertically into the dermis (which worsens prognosis).

![[page045_img01.jpeg]]
* **Visualize the three main layers: Epidermis (barrier), Dermis (structural/vascular), and Subcutis (fat).** 
* **Memorize the appendages located in the dermis.** This includes hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands.
* **Physiological context:** Pathology is categorized by depth. Eczema affects the epidermis (spongiosis); Scleroderma affects the dermis (collagen); Erythema Nodosum affects the subcutis.

![[page046_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Erythema: Redness of the skin that blanches (turns white) when pressed.** This distinguishes it from purpura (bleeding into skin), which does not blanch.
* **Consider the timeline.** Drug exanthems (rashes) typically appear 7–10 days after starting a new medication.
* **Physiological context:** Erythema is caused by vasodilation of the superficial capillaries, usually mediated by histamine or inflammatory cytokines.

![[page047_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define a Papule: A solid, raised lesion <1 cm in diameter.** 
* **Distinguish from a vesicle.** Papules are solid (cells or connective tissue); vesicles are fluid-filled.
* **Physiological context:** In cross-section, you see an accumulation of inflammatory cells or metabolic deposits in the dermis pushing the epidermis upward, or thickening of the epidermis itself.

![[page048_img01.jpeg]]
* **Memorize the 5 P’s of Lichen Planus:** Pruritic, Purple, Polygonal, Planar, Papules.
* **Look for Wickham’s Striae.** These are fine white lace-like lines on the surface of the papules.
* **Physiological context:** This is an autoimmune interface dermatitis where T-cells attack the basal layer of the epidermis.

![[page049_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define a Nodule: A solid, raised lesion >1 cm in diameter.** 
* **Compare depth to a papule.** Nodules usually involve deeper dermis or subcutis, giving them a more significant "palpable" component than papules.
* **Physiological context:** In Hypertrophic Lichen Planus, chronic scratching leads to extreme thickening (hypertrophy) of the lesions, turning papules into larger, persistent nodules.

![[page050_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define a Plaque: A palpable, flat-topped lesion >1 cm in diameter.** 
* **Think of a plaque as a "plateau."** It is broad rather than deep.
* **Physiological context:** Plaques often form by the coalescence (merging) of multiple smaller papules, common in psoriasis and eczema.

![[page050_img02.jpeg]]
* **Note the extensor distribution.** Psoriasis classically affects the "outer" surfaces: knees, elbows, and scalp.
* **Observe the "micaceous" scale.** This resembles silvery mica; if picked, it causes pinpoint bleeding (Auspitz sign).
* **Simplification:** Eczema likes the flexures (inside elbows/knees); Psoriasis likes the extensors (outside elbows/knees).

![[page051_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define a Bulla (plural: Bullae): A fluid-filled blister >0.5 cm (or >1 cm depending on text) in diameter.** 
* **Identify the multidermatomal pattern.** While shingles (Herpes Zoster) is usually a single dermatome, immunocompromised patients may show dissemination.
* **Physiological context:** Viral replication causes cell death and fluid accumulation between skin layers.

![[page052_img01.jpeg]]
* **Distinguish Vesicle vs. Bulla strictly by size.** Vesicle <0.5 cm; Bulla >0.5 cm.
* **Locate the split.** Blisters can be intra-epidermal (e.g., eczema, pemphigus) or sub-epidermal (e.g., bullous pemphigoid).
* **Physiological context:** The location of the split determines fragility. Intra-epidermal blisters break easily; sub-epidermal blisters are tense and sturdy.

![[page053_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Lichenification: Thickening of the skin with accentuated skin markings.** It looks like tree bark or leather.
* **Recognize the cause.** This is *always* secondary to chronic scratching or rubbing.
* **Physiological context:** It is a protective response of the skin (hypertrophy) to mechanical trauma. Treat the itch to stop the scratch.

![[page054_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Discoid (Nummular): Coin-shaped or disc-shaped lesions.** 
* **Differentiate from Ringworm (Tinea).** Discoid eczema is usually uniformly red/scaly; Tinea often has central clearing.
* **Tip:** "Nummular" comes from the Latin for coin. These are often intensely itchy and weepy.

![[page055_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define a Pustule: A circumscribed, elevated lesion containing purulent fluid (pus).** 
* **Note that pus does not always mean infection.** Pustules can be sterile (as in pustular psoriasis or some drug reactions).
* **Physiological context:** Pus is a collection of neutrophils (white blood cells). In this case, it is an intense inflammatory reaction to Argan oil.

![[page056_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Atrophy: Thinning of the epidermis and/or dermis.** 
* **Look for the "cigarette paper" sign.** The skin wrinkles easily when compressed and may look shiny or translucent.
* **Physiological context:** Lichen Sclerosus causes sclerosis (scarring) of the dermis and atrophy of the epidermis, often affecting genital skin but seen here extragenitally.

![[page057_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Ulceration: Full-thickness loss of the epidermis and at least part of the dermis.** 
* **Remember: Ulcers always heal with scarring.** This distinguishes them from erosions.
* **Physiological context:** Pyoderma Gangrenosum is a neutrophilic disease causing rapid, painful ulceration with undermined purple borders, often associated with IBD (Crohn's/UC).

![[page058_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Erosion: Loss of part or all of the epidermis only.** The basement membrane remains intact.
* **Remember: Erosions heal without scarring.** They may leave pigment changes, but the texture returns to normal.
* **Physiological context:** In blistering diseases like Pemphigoid, when the blister roof peels off, an erosion is left behind.

![[page059_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Excoriation: A linear erosion induced by scratching.** 
* **Look for linear crusts.** This confirms the patient is scratching, even if they deny it.
* **Physiological context:** In atopic dermatitis, the barrier is defective, leading to itch. The "itch-scratch cycle" leads to excoriation, which introduces bacteria (Staph aureus).

![[page060_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Crust: Dried serum, blood, or purulent exudate on the skin surface.** 
* **Differentiate from Scale.** Scale is excess skin (keratin); Crust is dried fluid ("a scab").
* **Physiological context:** Pemphigus Vulgaris causes fragile blisters that rupture almost immediately, so patients present with crusts rather than intact blisters.

![[page062_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Desquamation: Peeling or shedding of the outermost layer of the epidermis (stratum corneum).** 
* **Context matters.** It can occur after scarlet fever, toxic shock syndrome, or severe drug reactions (like SJS/TEN).
* **Physiological context:** This represents the resolution phase of acute inflammation or direct toxicity where the dead skin layer is sloughed off.

![[page064_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Annular: Ring-shaped lesion with an active edge and central clearing.** 
* **Distinguish from Discoid.** Discoid is a filled circle (coin); Annular is a ring.
* **Physiological context:** In Neonatal Lupus, maternal antibodies (anti-Ro/La) cross the placenta, causing transient annular rashes on the baby's face/scalp.

![[page065_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Reticulate: A net-like or lacy pattern.** 
* **Associate with vascular issues.** This pattern (Livedo Reticularis) often suggests slow blood flow or vasculitis (inflammation of blood vessels).
* **Physiological context:** The pattern mirrors the vascular supply to the skin segments; obstruction leads to the net-like discoloration.

![[page067_img01.jpeg]]
* **Define Hypopigmentation: Loss of skin color (lighter than background), but not totally white (depigmented).** 
* **Differentiate from Vitiligo.** Vitiligo is depigmented (chalk white); hypopigmentation still has some pigment.
* **Physiological context:** Cutaneous T-Cell Lymphoma (Mycosis Fungoides) can present deceptively as vague, dry, lighter patches, often misdiagnosed as eczema or fungus for years.

![[page069_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Irritant Contact Dermatitis.** It is caused by direct chemical damage to the skin barrier (water, soap, food acids).
* **Note the location.** Hand dermatitis in chefs is common due to "wet work." It often spares the creases/web spaces (unlike scabies).
* **Physiological context:** Non-immunologic disruption of the stratum corneum barrier.

![[page070_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Allergic Contact Dermatitis.** This is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction.
* **Look for the specific pattern.** Cement contains chromates; the dermatitis appears where the cement touches the skin.
* **Physiological context:** Type IV Hypersensitivity reaction (T-cell mediated). It requires prior sensitization (exposure) before the rash appears on subsequent exposure.

![[page071_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify the Photodistribution.** The rash is limited to areas exposed to light (face, V of neck), sparing shadowed areas (under chin, behind ears).
* **Suspect Chronic Actinic Dermatitis.** A condition where patients become allergic to UV light or materials activated by light.
* **Tip:** Always check the "triangle of sparing" behind the earlobes to confirm photosensitivity.

![[page073_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Spongiosis (Histology).** The arrow points to a vesicle within the epidermis caused by edema between keratinocytes.
* **Link histology to clinical appearance.** Spongiosis is the microscopic hallmark of Eczema.
* **Physiological context:** Fluid pushes cells apart, stretching their connections (desmosomes) until they become visible as "spines" or rupture to form vesicles.

![[page074_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Target (Iris) Lesions.** Three zones: a dark/blistered center, a pale edematous ring, and a red outer ring.
* **Diagnosis:** Erythema Multiforme.
* **Physiological context:** A hypersensitivity reaction usually triggered by infections (Herpes Simplex Virus is #1) or drugs.

![[page075_img01.jpeg]]
* **Describe Herpes Simplex.** Grouped vesicles on an erythematous base ("bunch of grapes on a red plate").
* **Note the progression.** Vesicles rupture → erosions → crusts.
* **Physiological context:** Recurrent infection; the virus stays dormant in the dorsal root ganglion and reactivates during stress.

![[page075_img02.jpeg]]
* **Describe Bullous Pemphigoid.** Large, tense bullae that do not rupture easily.
* **Contrast with Pemphigus.** Pemphigus = flaccid blisters (intra-epidermal); Pemphigoid = tense blisters (sub-epidermal).
* **Physiological context:** Autoantibodies attack the basement membrane zone (hemidesmosomes), causing the whole epidermis to lift off.

![[page076_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Bullous Cellulitis.** Severe swelling from bacterial infection can cause secondary blistering.
* **Look for signs of infection.** Warmth, spreading redness, fever, unilateral presentation.
* **Physiological context:** Exotoxins from bacteria (like Strep) or severe edema from inflammation can cause epidermal cleavage.

![[page077_img01.jpeg]]
* **Identify Insect Bite Reactions (Papular Urticaria).** Often grouped or linear vesicles/bullae.
* **Look for the "Breakfast, Lunch, Dinner" sign.** 3 bites in a line (common with bedbugs or fleas).
* **Physiological context:** An exaggerated immune response to insect saliva.

![[page079_img01.jpeg]]
* **Understand Psoriasis Triggers.** Genetics (family history) + Environmental Triggers (Stress, Alcohol, Drugs like beta-blockers/lithium, Strep throat, Trauma).
* **Learn the Koebner Phenomenon.** Psoriasis lesions appearing at sites of physical trauma (scratches, surgical scars).
* **Simplification:** Psoriasis is a "loaded gun" (genetics); the environment "pulls the trigger."
