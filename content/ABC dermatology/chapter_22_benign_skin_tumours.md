### **Seborrhoeic Keratoses & Differential Diagnosis**

![[page636_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the multiple brown, warty papules (Seborrhoeic Keratoses) scattered on the back.
*   **The "Ugly Duckling" Sign:** The arrow points to a lesion that looks distinct from the others (a melanoma); it is darker, irregular, and lacks the "stuck-on" appearance.
*   **Explanation:** **Always look for the outlier.** Seborrhoeic Keratoses (SKs) are benign basaloid proliferations ("senile warts"). However, the presence of many benign lesions can mask a malignant one. The melanoma here is identified because it breaks the pattern of the surrounding benign "background noise."

![[page637_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Classic Appearance:** Observe the "stuck-on" appearance, looking like wax dropped on the skin.
*   **Surface Features:** Look for a verrucous (wart-like) surface and "comedo-like openings" (plugged follicles).
*   **Explanation:** SKs are the most common benign tumor in older adults. They can range from tan to black. The "stuck-on" quality is a key physical exam finding—you feel like you could peel them off with a fingernail (though you shouldn't).

![[page638_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Variant Identification:** Identify the multiple, small, hyperpigmented, smooth papules on the cheeks and face.
*   **Demographics:** This is **Dermatosis Papulosa Nigra (DPN)**, a variant of seborrhoeic keratosis common in Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI.
*   **Explanation:** Histologically identical to SKs but cosmetically distinct. **Tip:** Be very careful treating these (e.g., with cryotherapy) as freezing can cause permanent hypopigmentation (white spots) in darker skin tones.

### **Common Benign Growths & Solar Damage**

![[page639_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the soft, pedunculated (on a stalk), flesh-colored papules.
*   **Location:** These are **Acrochordons (Skin Tags)**, typically found in intertriginous areas (axilla, neck, groin, eyelids).
*   **Explanation:** These are benign outgrowths often associated with friction, obesity, and insulin resistance. A sudden eruption of many skin tags can be a marker for metabolic syndrome.

![[page640_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the multiple, well-demarcated, flat, brown macules on the upper back (sun-exposed area).
*   **Pathology:** These are **Solar Lentigines** (Liver spots/Age spots).
*   **Explanation:** Unlike freckles (ephelides)—which darken in the sun and are just increased pigment—lentigines are an actual hyperplasia (increase in number) of melanocytes due to cumulative UV damage. They do not fade significantly in winter.

### **Melanocytic Naevi (Moles)**

![[page641_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the large, distinct pigmented plaque present on the torso.
*   **Classification:** This is a **Congenital Melanocytic Naevus** (present at birth).
*   **Explanation:** These are hamartomas of the neural crest. They are classified by size (small <1.5cm, medium, large, giant >20cm). **Giant congenital naevi** carry a higher risk of malignant transformation to melanoma and require monitoring.

![[page644_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the slate-grey to blue, ill-defined patch on the buttocks/sacrum of an infant.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Mongolian Blue Spot** (Congenital Dermal Melanocytosis).
*   **Explanation:** The blue color is due to the **Tyndall Effect**: light scatters through the dermis, making deep pigment look blue. These usually fade during childhood. **Important:** Document these at birth so they are not mistaken for bruising (non-accidental injury) later.

![[page645_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Life Cycle of a Mole (Stage 1):** Note that the lesion is completely flat (macular) and uniformly brown.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Junctional Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** "Junctional" means the nests of melanocytes are located at the junction between the epidermis and dermis. Because they are superficial, they are flat and pigmented.

![[page646_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Life Cycle of a Mole (Stage 2):** The lesion is slightly raised (papular) but still pigmented.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Compound Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** "Compound" means melanocytic nests are found in *both* the epidermis (providing pigment) and the dermis (providing elevation/bulk).

![[page647_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Life Cycle of a Mole (Stage 3):** The lesion is dome-shaped, fleshy, and has lost most of its dark pigment.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Intradermal Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** The melanocytes have migrated entirely into the dermis. As moles age, they tend to lose their junctional (pigment) activity and become purely dermal (fleshy bumps).

### **Specialized & Distinctive Naevi**

![[page648_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the deep steel-blue to black, smooth, dome-shaped papule.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Blue Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** The pigment is located deep in the reticular dermis. The deeper the melanin, the bluer it looks (Tyndall effect). These are usually stable and benign.

![[page649_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the pinkish-red to brown, rapid-growing nodule, often on the face or limbs of a child.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Spitz Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** Historically called "benign juvenile melanoma" because the histology looks scary (spindle cells) and very similar to melanoma. While benign, they are often excised because it is difficult to distinguish them from melanoma with certainty.

![[page650_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the central mole surrounded by a ring of depigmentation (white skin).
*   **Diagnosis:** **Halo Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** This represents a T-cell mediated immune response destroying the melanocytes. The mole will eventually disappear. **Tip:** Check the rest of the skin; while usually benign, this immune reaction can sometimes be triggered by a melanoma elsewhere or vitiligo.

![[page651_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Identify the large, unilateral, hyperpigmented patch with increased hair growth (hypertrichosis) on the shoulder/upper trunk.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Becker’s Naevus**.
*   **Explanation:** This is actually an epidermal hamartoma (not a true melanocytic mole). It is androgen-dependent, which is why it typically becomes darker and hairy during puberty in males.

### **Vascular & Fibrous Tumours**

![[page652_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the firm, pink/brown dermal nodule, commonly on the legs.
*   **Clinical Sign:** This is a **Dermatofibroma**. The key diagnostic test is the **"Dimple Sign"** (pinching the lesion causes it to retract inward).
*   **Explanation:** It is a reactive proliferation of scar tissue/fibroblasts, often following a minor injury like an insect bite or ingrown hair.

![[page653_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the pale pink, flat macular stain on the nape of the neck (or glabella).
*   **Diagnosis:** **Naevus Flammeus Neonatorum** (Stork Mark / Angel Kiss).
*   **Explanation:** A transient capillary malformation. "Angel kisses" (face) usually fade; "Stork marks" (neck) often persist but are covered by hair. They are harmless.

![[page655_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the unilateral, dark red "Port Wine Stain" following the ophthalmic (V1) branch of the trigeminal nerve.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Sturge–Weber Syndrome**.
*   **Explanation:** This facial stain is a cutaneous marker for vascular malformations in the brain and eye. **Risk:** Glaucoma and seizures. Unlike salmon patches, Port Wine Stains do *not* fade and may thicken with age.

![[page657_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** (a) A bright red, lobulated "strawberry" plaque. (b) An ulcerated, deep cavernous haemangioma.
*   **Treatment:** **Infantile Haemangioma**. The ulcerated lesion is suitable for systemic **beta-blockers (Propranolol)**.
*   **Explanation:** These grow rapidly in the first months of life, then involute (shrink) over years. Ulceration is painful and risks scarring. Beta-blockers cause vasoconstriction and stop growth.

![[page658_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Identify the central red arteriole with radiating capillaries ("legs").
*   **Diagnosis:** **Spider Naevus (Spider Telangiectasia)**.
*   **Explanation:** Pressing the center blanches the whole lesion. Common in children, but in adults, multiple lesions can indicate **high estrogen states** (pregnancy, liver cirrhosis, oral contraceptive pill).

![[page659_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the small, bright red to purple, dome-shaped papules on the trunk.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Campbell de Morgan Spots (Cherry Angiomas)**.
*   **Explanation:** Extremely common benign capillary proliferations. They increase in number with age. They are of no medical consequence.

![[page660_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the rapidly growing, friable (easily bleeding), red vascular nodule, often with a "collarette" of scale at the base.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Pyogenic Granuloma**.
*   **Explanation:** A misnomer: it is neither pyogenic (infected) nor a granuloma. It is a lobular capillary hemangioma. Often triggered by trauma or pregnancy. Treatment usually requires curettage and cautery.

### **Adnexal (Gland/Hair) Tumours & Cysts**

![[page661_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the multiple, small, firm, skin-colored papules clustered around the eyelids.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Syringomas**.
*   **Explanation:** Benign tumors of the eccrine (sweat) ducts. They are purely cosmetic but difficult to treat without scarring.

![[page662_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Identify the flesh-colored, shiny papules in the nasolabial folds (smile lines).
*   **Diagnosis:** **Trichoepitheliomas**.
*   **Explanation:** Benign tumors of the hair follicle. They can look very similar to Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC). Multiple lesions may indicate a genetic syndrome (e.g., Brooke-Spiegler).

![[page663_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the translucent, cystic nodule with a bluish hue on the eyelid margin.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Apocrine Hidrocystoma**.
*   **Explanation:** A benign retention cyst of an apocrine sweat gland. The blue color is due to the Tyndall effect on the clear fluid inside.

![[page664_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the tiny (1-2mm), firm, pearly-white papules.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Milia**.
*   **Explanation:** These are tiny epidermoid cysts containing keratin, not pus. They can occur spontaneously or after trauma/blistering (e.g., sun damage, porphyria).

![[page665_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Identify the yellowish papules with central depression (umbilication) on the forehead.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Sebaceous Gland Hyperplasia**.
*   **Explanation:** Overgrown oil glands. **Distinction from BCC:** SGH is yellow (fatty) and the blood vessels wrap *between* the lobules, whereas BCC is pearly/pink with vessels crossing *over* the lesion.

![[page666_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the soft, rubbery, subcutaneous lump.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Lipoma**.
*   **Explanation:** A benign tumor of fat. **"Slip Sign":** If you press on the edge, the mass slips out from under your finger. They are mobile and distinct from the overlying skin.

![[page667_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the reddish-brown firm nodule.
*   **Clinical Pearl:** **Leiomyoma** (derived from the arrectores pilorum muscle). These are characteristically **painful** (often triggered by cold or pressure).
*   **Explanation:** Think of the mnemonic "LEND AN EGG" for painful skin tumors (L = Leiomyoma).

![[page668_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the dome-shaped nodule with a central black dot (**punctum**).
*   **Diagnosis:** **Epidermoid Cyst** (often wrongly called sebaceous cyst).
*   **Explanation:** The punctum is the blocked follicle opening. The cyst is lined with epidermis and filled with foul-smelling "cheesy" keratin. The entire sac must be removed to prevent recurrence.

![[page669_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the fleshy, red, vascular-looking nodule on the sole of the foot.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Eccrine Poroma**.
*   **Explanation:** A benign tumor derived from the intra-epidermal sweat duct (acrosyringium). Often mistaken for pyogenic granuloma or amelanotic melanoma.

### **Hamartomas & Scars**

![[page670_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the smooth, firm overgrowth of scar tissue on the earlobe.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Keloid Scar**.
*   **Explanation:** A hyper-response to trauma (piercing). **Key distinction:** Keloids extend *beyond* the original wound margins (unlike hypertrophic scars). They are notoriously difficult to treat and often recur after excision.

![[page671_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Identify the yellow-orange, waxy, hairless plaque on the scalp.
*   **Diagnosis:** **Naevus Sebaceus**.
*   **Explanation:** A congenital hamartoma. It is smooth in childhood but becomes bumpy/warty at puberty. **Risk:** Secondary tumors (like BCC or Trichoblastoma) can develop within the plaque in adulthood.

![[page672_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Observe the linear, warty (verrucous) brown plaque.
*   **Pattern:** **Epidermal Naevus** following **Blaschko’s Lines**.
*   **Explanation:** These lines represent the migration path of skin cells during embryonic development. The lesion is due to genetic mosaicism (a mutation in one cell line).

![[page674_img01.jpeg]]
*   **Identification:** Note the linear, red, scaly, and inflamed plaque following Blaschko's lines.
*   **Diagnosis:** **ILVEN (Inflammatory Linear Verrucous Epidermal Naevus)**.
*   **Explanation:** Distinct from a standard epidermal naevus because it is **red and intensely itchy (pruritic)**. It is often mistaken for linear psoriasis but is very resistant to treatment.
