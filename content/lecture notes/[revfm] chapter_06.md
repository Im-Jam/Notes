Here is a comprehensive study guide oriented for medical students, breaking down the pathology, physiology, and clinical management for each image.

![[page082_img01.png]]
* **Identify the diffuse punctate staining across the cornea and conjunctiva.** This pattern indicates widespread epithelial cell loss, characteristic of severe Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (Dry Eye Syndrome).
* **Understand the mechanism of fluorescein dye.** The dye does not stain healthy epithelium; it pools in gaps where cells are missing or damaged (desiccation), appearing green under cobalt blue light.
* **Physiological point regarding the tear film.** This presentation suggests a failure of the aqueous layer (lacrimal gland) or the mucin layer (goblet cells), leading to rapid tear evaporation and epithelial cell death.

![[page082_img02.png]]
* **Recognize the comma-shaped, mucus-draped strands attached to the corneal surface.** These are filaments composed of degenerated epithelial cells and mucus, known as Filamentary Keratitis.
* **Clinical tip on symptoms.** These filaments are attached to nerve-rich corneal epithelium; every time the patient blinks, the lid drags the filament, causing significant pain and foreign body sensation.
* **Management simplification.** Treatment often involves manually removing the filaments and using mucolytic agents (like acetylcysteine) to dissolve the excess mucus.

![[page083_img01.png]]
* **Observe the surgical closure of the outer (lateral) corners of the eyelids.** This procedure is a lateral tarsorrhaphy, performed to reduce the palpebral aperture (eye opening).
* **Physiological indication.** By narrowing the opening, you decrease the surface area available for evaporation. This is crucial for "Exposure Keratopathy," often caused by facial nerve palsy (CN VII) where the eye cannot close fully (lagophthalmos).
* **Study tip.** This is often a temporary measure to allow a persistent corneal epithelial defect to heal by shielding it from the environment.

![[page084_img05.png]]
* **Trace the anatomical flow of contrast in this Dacryocystogram (DCG).** The sequence (a to c) visualizes the patent drainage system: Lower Canaliculus $\rightarrow$ Common Canaliculus $\rightarrow$ Lacrimal Sac $\rightarrow$ Nasolacrimal Duct $\rightarrow$ Nose.
* **Diagnostic utility.** A DCG is an *anatomical* test. It helps surgeons locate exactly where a stenosis (narrowing) or stricture is occurring before performing surgery.
* **Anatomy refresher.** Remember the "Valve of Hasner" is at the distal end (in the nose); this is a common site for congenital obstruction in infants.

![[page084_img02.png]]
* **Contrast this image with the previous DCG; this is a Dacryoscintogram.** This uses a radioactive tracer (Technetium-99m) and a gamma camera to track tear flow over time, making it a *functional* test rather than just anatomical.
* **Interpret the asymmetry.** The left side shows dye in the nose (normal drainage). The right side shows dye pooling in the sac but not entering the duct, indicating a functional block at the sac-duct junction.
* **Physiological point.** This test is useful when the anatomy looks open (patent on syringing), but the patient still complains of tearing (epiphora), indicating the "lacrimal pump" mechanism is failing.

![[page084_img04.png]]
* **Confirm the diagnosis of right-sided nasolacrimal duct obstruction.** As noted in the previous image, the tracer accumulation in the sac without nasal passage confirms the obstruction is post-sac.
* **Clinical correlation.** A "functional block" means the system isn't pumping tears even if the tubes aren't physically glued shut. This often occurs due to lid laxity or weakness in the orbicularis oculi muscle (which drives the pump).

![[page085_img01.png]]
* **Visualize the mechanism of a Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR).** The goal is to bypass the blocked nasolacrimal duct by creating a new window (ostium) directly connecting the Lacrimal Sac to the Nasal Cavity.
* **Surgical anatomy.** The surgeon drills through the lacrimal bone to suture the mucosa of the lacrimal sac to the mucosa of the nose.
* **Simplification.** Think of this as "replumbing" the drain. Since the bottom pipe (nasolacrimal duct) is clogged, you drill a hole halfway down the pipe to let water drain sideways into the nose.

![[page085_img02.png]]
* **Identify the acute abscess formation at the medial canthus.** This is Acute Dacryocystitis, an infection of the lacrimal sac usually caused by Staph or Strep species.
* **Note the "pointing" through the skin.** This indicates the abscess is about to rupture spontaneously, which can form a lacrimal fistula (an abnormal tunnel from the sac to the skin).
* **Crucial safety tip.** Never probe the lacrimal system during acute infection, as you risk spreading the bacteria into the orbit (orbital cellulitis) or cavernous sinus. Treat with systemic antibiotics and warm compresses first.
