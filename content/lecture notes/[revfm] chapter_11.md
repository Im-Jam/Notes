Here is the comprehensive study guide based on the image collection, oriented to help you understand the pathophysiology and clinical signs of retinal and macular conditions.

![[page136_img02.png]]
* **Distinguish between Dry and Wet AMD.**
    *   **Dry AMD (Figure b):** Characterized by **drusen**—yellowish deposits of metabolic waste (lipofuscin) that accumulate between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Bruch’s membrane. It progresses slowly and causes atrophy.
    *   **Wet AMD (Figure c):** Characterized by **Choroidal Neovascularization (CNV)**. Abnormal blood vessels break through Bruch’s membrane, leaking fluid and blood (haemorrhage) under the retina, causing rapid vision loss and scarring.
* **Interpret the OCT scan (Figure d).**
    *   The Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) shows fluid (dark spaces) disrupting the retinal layers. In a healthy eye, the layers should be compact; here, the fluid indicates active leakage (exudative/wet AMD) requiring anti-VEGF treatment.

![[page137_img01.png]]
* **Identify Angioid Streaks.**
    *   These jagged, irregular lines radiating from the optic disc resemble blood vessels but are actually cracks in a brittle **Bruch's membrane**.
* **Understand the systemic association.**
    *   These are pathognomonic for **Pseudoxanthoma Elasticum (PXE)**, a connective tissue disorder affecting skin and elastic arteries. The clinical risk is that new blood vessels (CNV) can grow through these cracks, leading to secondary wet macular degeneration.

![[page137_img02.png]]
* **Recognize the geometry of a Macular Hole.**
    *   A full-thickness defect in the fovea leads to a central blind spot (scotoma) and distortion (metamorphopsia).
* **Compare the OCT findings.**
    *   **Normal (c):** Shows a smooth foveal depression (pit).
    *   **Hole (b):** Shows a complete "missing piece" of the neurosensory retina with steep edges. This is usually caused by **vitreomacular traction**—the vitreous gel pulling too hard on the macula as it tries to separate.

![[page138_img02.jpeg]]
* **Identify Central Serous Retinopathy (CSR).**
    *   This condition typically affects young, "Type A" males or those on steroid therapy, presenting as a blister of fluid under the macula.
* **Analyze the fluid dynamics.**
    *   Unlike Wet AMD, there is usually **no blood**. The OCT (b) shows a clean, dome-shaped elevation of the neurosensory retina detached from the RPE by serous fluid. It often resolves spontaneously but can become chronic.

![[page139_img01.png]]
* **Differentiate Macular Oedema from detachment.**
    *   **Macular Oedema:** Fluid accumulates *within* the retinal layers (intra-retinal), often forming cystic spaces (Cystoid Macular Oedema - CME).
    *   **Detachment/CSR:** Fluid is *under* the retina (sub-retinal).
* **Understand the "Honeycomb" appearance.**
    *   On the OCT (b), note the black, empty cystic spaces separated by retinal tissue columns. This disruption of retinal architecture causes blurred central vision and is common in diabetic retinopathy, vein occlusions, and post-cataract surgery (Irvine-Gass syndrome).

![[page139_img02.png]]
* **Recognize the Bull’s-eye Maculopathy.**
    *   This specific pattern of concentric rings (central hyperpigmentation surrounded by a ring of hypopigmentation) is a hallmark of toxicity.
* **Recall the causative agent.**
    *   This is classically caused by **Chloroquine or Hydroxychloroquine** (Plaquenil) toxicity. The damage is irreversible, making regular screening (visual fields/OCT) essential for patients on these long-term medications.

![[page140_img01.png]]
* **Visualize Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD).**
    *   This is a normal aging change where the vitreous gel liquefies and shrinks, peeling away from the retina.
* **Identify the risk of traction.**
    *   The ultrasound shows the vitreous membrane separated from the retina but still attached at the optic disc (a strong adherence point). While PVD is benign (causing floaters), the mechanical pulling during separation can tear the retina.

![[page141_img01.png]]
* **Understand Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment (RRD).**
    *   "Rhegma" means break; this detachment is caused by a tear in the retina allowing liquefied vitreous fluid to pass through.
* **Trace the sequence of events.**
    *   (1) Vitreous pulls on retina (Traction) → (2) Retina tears → (3) Fluid enters the sub-retinal space → (4) The neurosensory retina peels off the RPE. This separates the photoreceptors from their blood supply (the choroid), constituting a medical emergency.

![[page142_img01.png]]
* **Spot the clinical signs of Detachment.**
    *   The detached retina looks grey, opaque, and crinkled (like a curtain), unlike the pink transparency of attached retina.
* **Locate the "Culprit" tear.**
    *   The arrow points to the retinal tear (horseshoe tear). Finding and sealing this break is the primary goal of surgery; if the break isn't sealed, the fluid will continue to re-detach the retina.

![[page143_img01.png]]
* **Compare Surgical Approaches.**
    *   **External (Scleral Buckle):** A silicone band is sutured to the outside of the eye to indent the wall (b), pushing the RPE back into contact with the detached retina to relieve traction.
    *   **Internal (Vitrectomy):** The vitreous gel is removed (c), and a gas bubble is injected to act as an internal splint (tamponade), holding the retina flat while a laser barrier heals around the tear.

![[page144_img01.png]]
* **Identify Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP).**
    *   Look for the classic triad: **Bone-spicule pigmentation** (clumps of pigment in the periphery), waxy optic disc pallor, and attenuated (thin) vessels.
* **Understand the functional loss.**
    *   RP is a hereditary degeneration of **Rod photoreceptors** first. This leads to night blindness (nyctalopia) followed by a progressive loss of peripheral vision (tunnel vision), eventually affecting central vision.

![[page145_img01.png]]
* **Recognize Ocular Albinism.**
    *   The image demonstrates **Iris Transillumination**, where light reflects off the retina and passes back through the iris due to a lack of melanin pigment.
* **Connect pigment to visual development.**
    *   Melanin is required for proper foveal development. Patients with albinism typically have **foveal hypoplasia** (leading to poor visual acuity/nystagmus) and significant photophobia due to light scattering.

![[page146_img01.png]]
* **Assess Leucocoria (White Pupil).**
    *   A white reflex instead of the normal red reflex in a child is a "red flag" requiring immediate referral.
* **List the differentials.**
    *   While it could be a congenital cataract or Coats' disease, you must rule out **Retinoblastoma**, a life-threatening intraocular tumor. Early detection is vital for survival and saving the eye.

![[page146_img02.png]]
* **Identify Retinal Astrocytoma.**
    *   This is a benign, calcified tumor that often has a "mulberry" appearance (white/yellow nodular mass).
* **Check for systemic disease.**
    *   While they can occur largely in isolation, they are strongly associated with **Tuberous Sclerosis** (Bourneville's disease). Diagnostic workup should look for systemic signs like ash-leaf spots or seizures.

![[page147_img01.png]]
* **Characterize CHRPE (Congenital Hypertrophy of RPE).**
    *   These are flat, dark, well-demarcated lesions often described as "bear tracks."
* **Assess the risk.**
    *   Solitary lesions are benign and common. However, **multiple, bilateral lesions** are associated with **Gardner’s Syndrome** (Familial Adenomatous Polyposis), which carries a 100% risk of colon cancer; these patients need a GI referral.

![[page147_img02.png]]
* **Distinguish Benign vs. Malignant.**
    *   **(a) CHRPE:** Note the depigmented halo or "comet tail"—this is a sign of a benign, stationary lesion.
    *   **(b) Choroidal Melanoma:** Note the elevation and orange pigment (lipofuscin). These are malignant.
* **Use the mnemonic "To Find Small Ocular Melanomas".**
    *   Suspicious signs include: **T**hickness (>2mm), **F**luid (subretinal), **S**ymptoms (flashes/floaters), **O**range pigment, and **M**argin (close to optic disc).
