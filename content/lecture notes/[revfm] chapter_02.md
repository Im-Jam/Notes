* **Distinguish between conjunctival injection and subconjunctival hemorrhage.** (a) Shows dilated, red vessels (injection) indicating inflammation, while (b) shows a solid sheet of blood trapped under the conjunctiva.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Physiology:** In image (a), the redness is caused by vasodilation in response to irritation (here, a corneal ulcer). This usually blanches with phenylephrine drops. In image (b), a vessel has ruptured, similar to a bruise on the skin.
    *   **Clinical Tip:** Subconjunctival hemorrhages look scary to patients but are usually painless and harmless (resolving in 1-2 weeks). Conversely, the ciliary flush seen in (a) associated with an ulcer is usually painful and threatens vision.

![[page040_img01.png|500]]
* **Identify "Cobblestone" Papillae as a hallmark of severe allergy.** These are giant, elevated hexagonal bumps found on the upper tarsal conjunctiva (under the upper lid).
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Diagnosis:** This is specific to **Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC)**, a chronic allergic condition common in young males.
    *   **Physiology:** The bumps are accumulations of inflammatory cells and collagen.
    *   **Simplification:** Think of these as giant hives on the inside of the eyelid. You must flip the eyelid to see them!

![[page040_img02.png|500]]
* **Differentiate Ectropion (turning out) from Entropion (turning in).** (a) The lower lid sags outward, exposing the conjunctiva; (b) the lid margin rolls inward, pushing lashes against the eye.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Consequences:**
        *   **Ectropion:** Causes tearing (epiphora) because the punctum (tear drain) is no longer in contact with the tear lake.
        *   **Entropion:** Causes **Trichiasis** (lashes rubbing the cornea), leading to pain, abrasions, and potential scarring.
    *   **Etiology:** Both are commonly caused by age-related laxity of the eyelid tendons (canthal tendons).

![[page045_img01.png|500]]
* **Recognize the hierarchy of Visual Acuity testing charts.** Use Snellen (a) or logMAR (b) for literate adults, and Preferential Looking cards (c, d) for infants.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Physiology:** Visual acuity tests the resolving power of the fovea.
    *   **Extra Tip:** The logMAR chart (b) is the modern gold standard because the spacing between letters is uniform, and every line has the same number of letters, preventing the "crowding phenomenon" from skewing results in conditions like amblyopia (lazy eye).
    *   **Pediatrics:** Infants (c, d) will naturally look at a pattern (stripes/shapes) rather than a blank gray card. The finest stripe they look at determines their acuity.

![[page046_img01.png|500]]
* **Visualize the Visual Field as a "Hill of Vision."** The peak of the hill is the fovea (sharpest vision), and sensitivity drops off as you move toward the periphery.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Concept:** The "sea of darkness" surrounds this hill. The diagram in (b) maps "Isoptres"—lines connecting points of equal retinal sensitivity (like altitude lines on a map).
    *   **Anatomy:** Note the blind spot (the white oval on the left of the plot). This corresponds to the optic nerve head, where there are no photoreceptors.

![[page047_img01.png|500]]
* **Interpret a Kinetic (Goldmann) Visual Field plot.** This shows a **Right Hemianopia**, where the patient has lost vision in the right half of the world in that eye.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Technique:** In kinetic perimetry, a light is moved from the non-seeing periphery toward the center until the patient sees it.
    *   **Localization:** A defect that respects the vertical midline (like this one) suggests a neurological lesion (e.g., stroke or tumor) located **behind the optic chiasm** in the brain.

![[page048_img01.png|500]]
* **Analyze an Automated Static Perimetry (Humphrey) printout.** Look for the "Probability Maps" (bottom row) to identify the depth and location of the defect (scotoma).
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Diagnosis:** The dark cluster of points in the superior field represents a glaucomatous defect.
    *   **Physiology:** In glaucoma, nerve fiber bundles die in an arc shape. Consequently, the visual field loss often follows an arc shape (Arcuate or Bjerrum scotoma) and respects the **horizontal** midline (unlike strokes, which respect the vertical).

![[page049_img01.png|500]]
* **Use Ishihara plates to screen for Red-Green color deficiency.** The patient must identify the number hidden within the colored dots.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Physiology:** These are "pseudo-isochromatic" plates. They rely on the confusion lines of the color spectrum for protan (red) and deutan (green) defects.
    *   **Limitation:** This test is excellent for congenital (genetic) color blindness in males but does *not* reliably detect Blue-Yellow defects (which are often acquired from diseases like glaucoma).

![[page049_img02.png|500]]
* **Assess Contrast Sensitivity using the Pelli-Robson chart.** Unlike acuity charts, the letters stay the same size but become progressively fainter (lower contrast).
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Clinical Value:** A patient with cataracts or corneal edema may read 20/20 on a black-and-white Snellen chart but fail miserably here.
    *   **Simplification:** This measures "quality" of vision (seeing in fog/dim light) rather than just "quantity" (reading small letters).

![[page050_img01.png|500]]
* **Understand the endpoint of Goldmann Applanation Tonometry.** The intraocular pressure (IOP) is read when the inner edges of the two fluorescein semi-circles (mires) just touch.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Physics:** This technique relies on the Imbert-Fick law. It measures the force required to flatten a fixed area of the cornea.
    *   **Technical Tip:** If the semicircles are too thick, you used too much fluorescein (reading will be falsely high). If they are too thin, you used too little (reading will be falsely low).

* **Perform the "Swinging Flashlight Test" to detect an RAPD.** If the pupil *dilates* when the light swings from the healthy eye to the affected eye, a Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect is present.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Physiology:** The brain perceives the light in the bad eye (e.g., Optic Neuritis) as "dimmer" than the good eye. Therefore, the brain sends a signal to dilate the pupil to let more light in, even though the flashlight is shining directly on it.
    *   **Key Concept:** An RAPD indicates damage to the **Optic Nerve** (before the chiasm). It does *not* occur in cataracts or refractive error.

![[page052_img02.png|500]]
* **Isolate Levator function when assessing Ptosis.** You must firmly press on the brow (frontalis muscle) to prevent the patient from using their forehead to lift their eyelids.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Anatomy:** The *Levator palpebrae superioris* is the primary lid lifter. The *Frontalis* is an accessory lifter.
    *   **Clinical Tip:** If you don't block the brow, a patient with severe ptosis might look "normal" by constantly raising their eyebrows (a compensatory mechanism).

![[page053_img01.png|500]]
* **Use Fluorescein to diagnose surface defects and perforation.** (a, b) show corneal **abrasions** (staining green); (c, d) show a **Seidel Sign** (leaking fluid).
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Staining (b):** The dye stains the corneal stroma (hydrophilic) but slides off the intact epithelium (hydrophobic). If it glows, the "skin" of the eye is broken.
    *   **Seidel Sign (d):** Used to check for a perforated cornea. You apply concentrated (dark) dye. If aqueous humor leaks out, it dilutes the dye, causing a stream of bright green fluid to flow down the eye surface (like a waterfall).

![[page054_img01.png|500]]
* **Master the Eversion Technique for foreign body checks.** A cotton bud acts as a fulcrum at the lid crease to flip the tarsal plate.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Patient Instruction:** The single most important step is asking the patient to **look down**. This relaxes the Levator muscle; you cannot flip the lid if the patient is looking up or squeezing.

![[page054_img02.png|500]]
* **Adopt proper ergonomics for Direct Ophthalmoscopy.** Always use your Right eye to examine the patient's Right eye (and Left for Left).
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Why?** This prevents you from bumping noses with the patient ("cheek-to-cheek" position).
    *   **Optics:** This provides a high magnification (15x) upright image, but the field of view is very small (like looking through a keyhole). You must pivot the ophthalmoscope to see different parts of the retina.

![[page055_img01.png|500]]
* **Navigate the normal Fundus landmarks.** Locate the Optic Disc (nasal side), the Macula (temporal side, darker, no vessels), and the Vascular Arcades.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Normal Variant (b):** In young people, the fundus often has a "watered silk" shimmering reflection. This is a normal reflection from the Internal Limiting Membrane and should not be confused with edema.
    *   **Vessels:** Veins are darker and thicker; Arteries are lighter and thinner.

![[page056_img01.png|500]]
* **Utilize B-Scan Ultrasound when the view is blocked.** This acoustic imaging creates a cross-section of the globe, useful when cataracts or vitreous hemorrhage prevent ophthalmoscopy.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Orientation:** The lens is at the top (front), the vitreous is the dark black space in the middle (fluid), and the optic nerve is the "W" shaped shadow behind the eye.
    *   **Indication:** Crucial for ruling out **Retinal Detachment** in eyes with opaque media.

![[page057_img01.png|500]]
* **Interpret Corneal Topography heat maps.** "Red" represents steep curvature (high power), and "Blue" represents flat curvature.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Clinical Use:** Essential for diagnosing **Keratoconus** (where you would see a localized red cone, usually inferiorly) and for planning LASIK surgery.
    *   **Physics:** A steep curve (red) bends light more strongly than a flat curve.

* **Assess Retinal Vascular integrity with Fluorescein Angiography (FFA).** (a) shows normal vessel filling; (b) shows **Hyperfluorescence** (leakage) at the macula.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **The Process:** Dye is injected into a vein in the arm. It travels to the eye, lighting up retinal arteries first, then veins.
    *   **Pathology:** In (b), the dye is leaking out of damaged vessels into the retinal tissue. This confirms **Macular Edema** (swelling), common in Diabetic Retinopathy.

![[page058_img04.png|500]]
* **Use OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography) for a "Virtual Biopsy."** This scan creates a high-resolution cross-section of the retinal layers.
* **Explanation:**
    *   **Anatomy:** The central dip is the **Foveal Pit** (normal anatomy).
    *   **Pathology:** If this pit is gone or elevated, it indicates macular edema. If the layers are disrupted, it may indicate a macular hole.
    *   **Technology:** It uses light waves (interferometry) to measure distance, similar to how ultrasound uses sound, but with micrometer precision.
