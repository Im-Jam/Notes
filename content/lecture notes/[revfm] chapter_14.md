* **Master the anatomy of the visual pathway to predict lesion sites.**
    *   **Explanation:** The visual pathway is topologically organized. A lesion at the **optic nerve** affects only that eye (monocular blindness). A lesion at the **optic chiasm** affects crossing fibers from the nasal retina (which view the temporal field), causing **bitemporal hemianopia** (tunnel vision). Lesions **behind the chiasm** (tract, radiation, cortex) cause **contralateral homonymous hemianopia** (loss of the same side of the visual field in both eyes).
* **Understand the difference between Temporal and Parietal lobe radiations.**
    *   **Explanation:** Fibers radiating through the **Temporal lobe** (Meyer's loop) carry information from the inferior retina (superior visual field); a lesion here causes a "Pie in the Sky" defect (superior quadrantanopia). Fibers in the **Parietal lobe** carry superior retinal info (inferior visual field); a lesion here causes a "Pie on the Floor" defect (inferior quadrantanopia).
* **Remember the "Macular Sparing" rule for cortical lesions.**
    *   **Explanation:** Lesions in the visual cortex (occipital lobe) often result in hemianopia but spare the central vision (macula). This is because the macula has a vast representation in the cortex and often receives a dual blood supply (PCA and MCA), protecting it from strokes that affect peripheral vision.

* **Distinguish "Papilloedema" from other causes of disc swelling.**
    *   **Explanation:** Medically, the term **Papilloedema** is reserved *exclusively* for optic disc swelling caused by raised **intracranial pressure** (usually bilateral). Swelling from other causes (like inflammation or ischemia) should be termed "optic disc edema" or "swollen disc" to avoid confusion during diagnosis.
* **Categorize causes into Intracranial, Vascular, and Inflammatory.**
    *   **Explanation:** When you see a swollen disc, run through the mechanism checklist: Is it pressure from the brain (Tumor/Hypertension - Papilloedema)? Is it a lack of blood flow (AION/Diabetes - Vascular)? Is it inflammation of the nerve itself (Optic Neuritis/MS - Inflammatory)?
* **Don't forget "Pseudo-papilloedema" (Optic Disc Drusen).**
    *   **Explanation:** Sometimes the disc *looks* swollen but is actually calcified or elevated due to structural anomalies like Drusen. This is benign. Differentiate this using Ultrasound (B-scan) to look for calcium deposits.

* **Identify the hallmark field defect of Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (AION).**
    *   **Explanation:** AION typically presents with an **Altitudinal Defect**, where the upper or lower half of the visual field is suddenly lost (respecting the horizontal midline). This occurs because the blood supply to the optic nerve head is segmented into superior and inferior halves; if one half loses perfusion, that corresponding half of the visual field goes dark.
* **Differentiate Arteritic (AAION) from Non-Arteritic (NAION).**
    *   **Explanation:** This is a critical clinical fork in the road. **Non-arteritic** is usually due to hypertension/diabetes/sleep apnea ("stroke of the optic nerve"). **Arteritic** is caused by Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA). GCA is a medical emergency requiring high-dose steroids to prevent permanent blindness in the fellow eye. Always ask about scalp tenderness and jaw claudication!

* **Recognize Optic Atrophy by the color of the neuroretinal rim.**
    *   **Explanation:** A normal disc is pink/orange because it is perfused by tiny capillaries nourishing the axons. In **Optic Atrophy**, the nerve fibers die and the capillaries regress, leaving the disc looking pale, white, or "chalky."
* **Compare the "Cup-to-Disc" ratio and color simultaneously.**
    *   **Explanation:** In Glaucoma, the cup gets bigger (cupping), but the remaining rim often stays pink until late stages. In Optic Atrophy (neurological causes like MS or compression), the rim itself loses color (pallor) without necessarily cupping. *Rule of thumb: Pallor > Cupping = Neurological; Cupping > Pallor = Glaucoma.*
