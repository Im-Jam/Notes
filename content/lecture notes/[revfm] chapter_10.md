* **Observe the significant overlap between the Intraocular Pressure (IOP) distribution of the "normal" population and the "glaucomatous" population.**
    *   **Explanation:** This concept is crucial for diagnosis. It demonstrates that high IOP is a major risk factor, but not the *definition* of glaucoma. Many patients with high pressure never develop damage (Ocular Hypertension), while many patients with "normal" pressure develop optic nerve damage (Normal Tension Glaucoma).

![[page123_img01.png]]
* **Identify the irregular, meandering blood vessels growing on the surface of the iris (Rubeosis Iridis).**
    *   **Explanation:** This is a sign of severe retinal ischemia (often due to Diabetes or Central Retinal Vein Occlusion). These vessels are driven by VEGF; if they grow into the drainage angle, they "zip it shut," causing Neovascular Glaucoma—a very aggressive and painful form of secondary angle-closure glaucoma.

![[page124_img02.jpeg]]
* **Compare the healthy pink rim of the normal disc (a) to the thinned neuroretinal rim and enlarged "cup" of the glaucomatous disc (b).**
    *   **Explanation:** Glaucoma is defined by the death of retinal ganglion cells. As these nerve fibers die, the central empty space (the cup) gets larger while the neural tissue (the rim) gets thinner, increasing the "cup-to-disc ratio."
* **Look for the "Drance hemorrhage" or splinter hemorrhage at the disc margin in image (c).**
    *   **Explanation:** This small bleed is a red flag for unstable, progressing glaucoma. It is particularly common in Normal Tension Glaucoma and indicates that the current treatment is insufficient.
* **Identify the "Notch" in image (d), a focal area where the neuroretinal rim is completely absent.**
    *   **Explanation:** This represents severe localized damage. Because nerve fibers travel in specific bundles, a notch inferiorly on the nerve will correspond to a dense visual field defect in the superior field.

* **Examine the OCT scan (c), specifically the graph showing the Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) thickness falling into the "red zone."**
    *   **Explanation:** The red zone indicates thickness in the bottom 1% of the population (pathological). Structural damage (thinning of the nerve layer) usually occurs *before* the patient notices vision loss or fails a visual field test (Pre-perimetric glaucoma).
* **Correlate the inferior structural defect on the optic nerve with the scan data.**
    *   **Explanation:** The diagrams show that damage to the inferior aspect of the optic nerve results in superior visual field loss. This inverse relationship is due to the optics of the eye and the anatomical path of the nerve fiber bundles.

![[page126_img01.png]]
* **(Continued from previous figure) - Focus on the correlation between the "double hump" OCT graph and the optic nerve anatomy.**
    *   **Explanation:** A healthy RNFL graph (top right) has a "double hump" shape because nerve fibers are thickest superiorly and inferiorly. In glaucoma, these humps are flattened (arrows), representing the loss of the superior and inferior arcuate bundles.

![[page126_img02.png]]
* **Memorize the classic "Arcuate" or "Bjerrum" scotoma seen in the top visual field (a).**
    *   **Explanation:** This comma-shaped black area typically arches over the center of vision. It connects the "blind spot" (physiologic blind spot) to the nasal midline.
* **Note the "Nasal Step" and the preservation of central vision until the end stage (b).**
    *   **Explanation:** Glaucomatous damage respects the horizontal midline (nasal step). Crucially, central fixation (the very middle crosshairs) is spared until late in the disease, which is why patients are often asymptomatic until they have severe "tunnel vision."

![[page128_img01.png]]
* **Trace the path of aqueous humor in a Trabeculectomy: Anterior Chamber → Iridectomy → Sclerostomy → Sub-scleral space → Sub-conjunctival space (Bleb).**
    *   **Explanation:** This surgery creates a controlled leak (fistula). The fluid doesn't leave the eye; it pools under the conjunctiva (forming a blister called a "bleb") and is absorbed into the systemic circulation.
* **Note the "Iridectomy" (hole in the iris) shown in the diagram.**
    *   **Explanation:** A small piece of iris is removed to prevent the iris tissue from being sucked into the new drainage hole and plugging it up.

![[page128_img02.png]]
* **Identify the "Bleb" in image (b) and the drainage tube shunt in image (c).**
    *   **Explanation:** The bleb appears as an elevated, avascular area on the superior conjunctiva. The tube shunt (c) is used in refractory cases (like neovascular glaucoma) where a standard trabeculectomy would likely scar shut; the tube physically keeps the channel open.

![[page129_img01.png]]
* **Recognize the classic signs of Acute Angle Closure: a cloudy cornea and a mid-dilated, vertically oval pupil.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is a medical emergency. The IOP is so high (often >50 mmHg) that fluid is forced into the cornea (causing edema/haze and "halos" around lights). The high pressure causes ischemia to the iris sphincter, leaving the pupil fixed in a semi-dilated position.

![[page130_img01.png]]
* **Identify "Buphthalmos" (Ox-eye), characterized by enlarged corneal diameter and hazy corneas in an infant.**
    *   **Explanation:** This is Primary Congenital Glaucoma. Unlike adults, an infant's sclera and cornea are elastic. High pressure causes the actual globe to stretch and enlarge. The classic triad of symptoms is Epiphora (tearing), Photophobia (light sensitivity), and Blepharospasm (squeezing eyes shut).

![[page131_img01.png]]
* **Analyze the graph showing ganglion cell number versus age: Glaucoma is essentially "accelerated aging" of the optic nerve.**
    *   **Explanation:** Everyone loses nerve cells as they age (black line). Glaucoma speeds this up (red line).
* **Understand the concept of "Symptom-free lifetime" regarding the green and purple lines.**
    *   **Explanation:** We cannot regrow dead nerves (we cannot go back up the Y-axis). The goal of treatment is to flatten the slope (make the line horizontal) so the patient reaches the end of their life (X-axis) before their vision reaches the "disability" threshold. If diagnosed too late (purple line), even perfect treatment might not save vision because the "reserve" was already too low.
